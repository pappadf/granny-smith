## 1. Overview

### 1.1 What the part is

The NCR 53C94, 53C95 and 53C96 are high-performance CMOS SCSI controllers conforming to ANSI
X3.131-1986 — NCR's "Advanced SCSI Controller" (ASC) family, a superset of the earlier 53C90
with additional commands, registers and an 18-bit DMA interface (16 data bits plus two parity
bits) [1]. The three parts share one register model and command set and differ only in
transceiver control and packaging: the 53C94 has on-chip 48 mA single-ended drivers in an
84-pin PLCC, the 53C95 drives external differential transceivers, and the 53C96 offers both
single-ended and differential modes in a single 100-pin QFP [1].

The family's defining idea is that common SCSI algorithms — arbitration, selection, message and
command transfer, disconnect, command complete — run **in silicon in response to a single
command byte**, with a 16-byte by 9-bit FIFO between the SCSI bus and the host through which
every command, data, status and message byte passes [1][2]. Sustained transfer rates are up to
5 MB/s asynchronous and 5 MB/s synchronous [1]; the later NCR 53CF94/96-2 "fast SCSI" variant
extends this to 10 MB/s synchronous at a 40 MHz clock [3]. The microprocessor bus is 8 bits;
the DMA bus is 8 or 16 bits, and the two may be strapped as one shared bus or two independent
buses [1].

In the Macintosh line this controller family replaced the NCR 5380 of the compact and early
expansion Macs (see [ncr-5380.md](ncr-5380.md)): where the 5380 leaves virtually all protocol
to the CPU, the 53C96-class part runs whole SCSI sequences on its own, and it is the first
Macintosh SCSI controller with a real DMA request/acknowledge interface. Apple's firmware
gives the wrapper a name of its own — the Quadra-generation ROM's power-on self-test calls the
chip "53C96 Turbo SCSI chip" [6], and Apple's internal header reserves `DAFB_SCSIch0` as the
"Turbo SCSI channel 0 control" register [6].

### 1.2 Three chips named "53C96" — a naming trap

Three distinct silicon parts answer to a "53C96"-ish name, and they are **not** interchangeable:

| Part | Vendor | What it is | Macintosh role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NCR 53C96** | NCR | 53C94 core + single-ended **and** differential transceiver control, 100-pin QFP, 10–25 MHz clock | The discrete controller of the Quadra generation |
| **AMD Am53C96** (and Am53C94) | AMD | Second source, "pin/function compatible with NCR53C94/53C96" [2]; same 53C94-class core, 10–25 MHz, max 5 MB/s sync — **not** a fast part | The SCSI cell inside the Curio combo chip of the AV Quadras and the first-generation Power Macintosh |
| **NCR 53CF96-2** | NCR | "Fast SCSI" variant (53CF94/96-2): FASTCLK/FASTSCSI modes, 24-bit transfer counter, chip-ID readout, 36–40 MHz clocks, 10 MB/s sync [3] | The discrete fast-internal-bus controller of the Power Macintosh 8100 |

The differences that matter to a programmer are enumerated in §2: the AMD part has no registers
at chip addresses $0D/$0E at all, renames several bits, and reserves the upper halves of
Config 3 and the Synchronous Offset register; the CF96-2 adds Config 4, the counter-high/chip-ID
register at $0E, and the extra Config 3 speed bits [1][2][3]. Apple's driver detects the CF96-2
by its chip ID (§4.4) and treats everything else as the common 53C94-class model [5].

### 1.3 Which machines carry it

**The Quadra generation.** Apple's SCSI Manager 96 driver and its SCSI Manager 4.3 successor
identify their 53C96-class controllers per platform [5][6]:

| Platform (Apple's name in the driver) | Machines | Controllers | SCSI clock |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quadra 700 | Macintosh Quadra 700 | one 53C96-class part | 25 MHz |
| Quadra 900 / 950 ("Q9x0") | Macintosh Quadra 900, 950 | two 53C96-class parts (internal + external buses) | 15.6672 MHz on the 25 MHz-CPU machines, 25 MHz on the 33 MHz-CPU machines |
| Zydeco | machine identity outside this page's evidence (§6) | — | 24.28416 MHz |
| Cyclone | Macintosh Quadra 840AV / Centris 660AV | Curio's Am53C94/96-class cell | 25 MHz or 24.576 MHz (the source table marks the value uncertain) |
| Wombat | a 68k platform using the "BIOS-style" SCSI Manager 96 variant (*inferred — unverified*: the platform descriptor and its driver type are in the source; the machine mapping is not) | — | — |

Clock values are Apple's own table in the hardware-init source, which lists each platform's CPU
and SCSI clocks side by side ("Quadra700 25/25; Quadra900 25/15.6672; … Cyclone 33/25 or 24.576
(which?); PDM-Curio 20; PDM-Fast 40") [5]. On the Quadra 900/950 pair the driver discriminates
the 25 MHz-CPU from the 33 MHz-CPU machine with a VIA2 speed bit and loads a different clock
conversion factor and selection timeout for each [5].

**The first-generation Power Macintosh (Apple codename PDM).** Every PDM machine carries one
SCSI controller inside **Curio** (AMD AM79C950, "CURIO-B1"), the multipurpose I/O chip
combining a MACE Ethernet controller, a SCSI controller and an SCC serial controller [4]. The
Curio SCSI cell is the AMD Am53C94/96-class core (§1.2); the ROM's bus-inquiry interface
reports the controller family as "NCR 53C96" for it (*observed* in the ROM [7]). The Power
Macintosh 8100 adds a second, discrete **NCR 53CF96** (U34) driving a dedicated fast internal
bus [8]. The machines [4][5][7]:

| Machine | Controllers | Buses | SCSI clock |
|---|---|---|---|
| Power Macintosh 6100/60, /60AV, /66 | Curio SCSI cell only | 1 — standard 5 MB/s, internal 50-pin + external DB-25, continuous | 20 MHz |
| Power Macintosh 7100/66, /66AV, /80 | Curio SCSI cell only | 1 — as above | 20 MHz |
| Power Macintosh 8100/80, /80AV, /100, /110 | Curio SCSI cell + discrete NCR 53CF96 | 2 — the Curio's standard bus **plus** a fast internal 10 MB/s bus with its own 50-pin header | 20 MHz (Curio) + 40 MHz (53CF96) |

The Developer Note states the split plainly: "In the Power Macintosh 6100/60, 6100/60AV,
7100/66, and 7100/66AV, the fast internal SCSI bus is omitted" [4] p. 12; the 8100/80 and
8100/80AV "provide a fast SCSI interface (up to 10 MB per second) for internal SCSI devices, in
addition to the standard SCSI interface" [4] p. 9, p. 29. The standard bus is continuous
across the internal cable, the logic-board traces and the external DB-25; the 8100's fast bus
is driven separately and separately terminated [4] pp. 29–33. The AV configurations are
identical for SCSI (the AV card changes video and DSP only), and the enhanced 1994 models
(6100/66, 7100/80, 8100/100, 8100/110) make no SCSI changes at all — the *Enhanced Power
Macintosh Computers* developer note contains no SCSI discussion [9].

Both controllers answer at fixed base addresses decoded by the platform's I/O chip (§2.1); the
ROM's platform decoder tables give the internal controller first, so **SCSI Manager bus 0 is
the fast internal bus and bus 1 the Curio's standard bus on the 8100**, while on the 6100 and
7100 the one Curio bus is bus 0 [5][7].

### 1.4 Electrical form

All Macintosh installations are **single-ended**: the 53C96's and 53CF96's differential
transceiver-control capability is unused — on the 8100 the 53CF96's DIFFM strap is tied off
(*observed* in the schematic [8]). The chips' 48 mA open-drain SCSI drivers connect directly
to the bus [1]. Termination is automatic and has no programming model: on the Power Macintosh,
special circuitry terminates the standard bus at the external connector when no external
device is connected, the internal end is terminated in the last internal drive, and the fast
bus carries always-on board-end active termination [4] pp. 33–34, [8]. TERMPWR reaches the
DB-25 through a diode, a fuse and a ferrite [8]. The Developer Note allows up to seven SCSI
devices total per bus [4] p. 29.

### 1.5 Clocking

The chip's CLK input generates all internal timing; it must be programmed into the Clock
Conversion Factor register before any timed operation (§2.3, §3.5). The 53C94/95/96 and the
AMD parts accept 10–25 MHz (12 MHz minimum for synchronous operation); the 53CF96-2 accepts up
to 40 MHz with a special clock-factor encoding [1][2][3].

On the PDM machines the two controllers are clocked from the same oscillator tree: a 40.000 MHz
oscillator (G7) feeds the 53CF96's CK pin directly, and a 74F74 flip-flop divides it by two to
20 MHz for the Curio SCSI cell (*observed* in the 8100 schematics, sheets 11, 13, 14 [8]). The
Developer Note's fixed-clock table lists a 20.0000 MHz "Ethernet" oscillator serving "Ethernet,
SCSI" on all models [4] Table 2-3 pp. 19–20 — on the 6100/7100, whose schematics are not in
the evidence set, that oscillator is *inferred* to be the Curio SCSI clock source, while the
8100 demonstrably uses the divided 40 MHz arrangement. Apple's driver hard-codes the pair:
"Curio is running at 20MHz" loads clock factor 4; the fast bus loads the 36–40 MHz factor [5].

## 2. Register file

### 2.1 Address decode on the Mac

Inside the chip, four register-select lines A3–A0 choose one of sixteen register slots; the
read and write strobes pick which of the two registers sharing a slot answers [1][2]. On the
Mac the host bus wires those select lines to address bits 4–7 (or 4–8 for the Curio, whose
select space is shared across its SCSI, Ethernet and SCC cells), so **chip register N appears
at `base + N × $10`** — a 16-byte stride — and accesses are byte-wide on the low data lane
(*observed* in the schematics, sheets 12–14 [8]; the stride is stated outright in Apple's
driver: "offset of $10 between regs" [5]).

On the PDM machines the base addresses live in the $5000 0000–$5FFF FFFF I/O space decoded by
the AMIC chip [4] Table 2-5 p. 22:

| Address | What | Models |
|---|---|---|
| **$50F1 0000** | Curio SCSI cell register file (16-byte stride) | all |
| **$50F1 0100** | Curio pseudo-DMA aperture (§3.8) | all |
| **$50F1 1000** | 53CF96 register file (16-byte stride) | 8100 only |
| **$50F1 1100** | 53CF96 pseudo-DMA aperture | 8100 only |

The values come from the ROM's platform decoder tables — the 6100/7100 table lists the internal
SCSI96 at $50F10000 and the 8100 ("Cold Fusion") table lists the internal SCSI **CF**96 at
$50F11000 with the external SCSI C96 at $50F10000 — and the same pairs appear byte-for-byte in
the shipping ROM image (decoder tables at ROM offsets ≈ $15558/$155C4) [5][7]. Apple's HAL
derives the pseudo-DMA address as `pdmaAddr = baseRegAddr + $100` [5], which is how the
aperture offsets above arise.

Registers respond to byte accesses; the count, configuration and command registers are the
ones the Mac firmware actually exercises (§4). The rest of each $1000 page is undecoded — and
on the PDM platform an access the AMIC does not decode makes the AMIC assert an error signal
after 40 µs, a condition "not generally recoverable" [4] pp. 21–22. The SCSI subsystem
nevertheless *relies* on recoverable failures inside the aperture: see §3.8.

### 2.3 Per-register detail

#### Transfer Count / Transfer Counter ($0, $1; $E with Features Enable)

Written, these two registers form the 16-bit **count**: the number of bytes a DMA operation
will move. Read, they return the live **counter**, which decrements as bytes move. Any DMA
command (including the DMA NOP, $80) copies count into counter; a non-DMA NOP does not [1][2].

- The written count survives counter decrement and every reset (its power-up value is
  unpredictable); blocks of equal size can therefore be re-run without reprogramming [1][2].
- **Zero means 65536.** On the 53CF96-2 with Features Enable set, register $E extends the
  count/counter to 24 bits and zero means 16 MB [3]; Apple's PDM driver never uses this and
  leaves the 16-bit counter in force [5].
- The counter counts **bytes**, one per byte transfer or two per word transfer, and decrements
  on the leading edge of: DACK/ in target data-in and initiator synchronous data-in; REQO/ in
  target data-out; ACKO/ in initiator asynchronous data-in; DACK/ in initiator data-out [1].
- A false DACK/ — a cycle with no read or write strobe — decrements the counter anyway, out of
  sync with the data stream. When false DACKs can interfere with a suspended transfer, the
  DREQ high-impedance bit (Config 2 bit 4) makes the chip ignore DACK/ entirely [1].
- Two non-DMA paths also use the counter: during bus-initiated selection and the target
  receive-command-sequence, the chip decodes the CDB group code and loads the counter with the
  CDB length [1].

The Mac driver reads the counter back to compute residuals: bytes transferred = programmed
count − counter remainder (§4.7) [5].

#### FIFO ($2)

A 16-byte by 9-bit first-in-first-out (8 data bits plus a parity bit), reachable from three
buses: the SCSI bus, the microprocessor bus (through CS/ and the register address) and the DMA
bus (through DACK/, address ignored) [1][2]. On the AMD part the FIFO register is the only
register that can also be accessed via DACK [2]. The bottom FIFO element and the FIFO flags
are initialized to zero by hardware reset, software reset chip, **and the start of every
bus-initiated selection or reselection** [1][2]; the rest of the FIFO contents survive every
reset [1].

#### Command ($3)

A **two-deep** command FIFO. A second command may be stacked while the first is still
executing; Reset Chip, Reset SCSI Bus and Target Stop DMA bypass the queue and execute
immediately, all others wait. Reading the register returns the currently executing (or last
executed) command and has no side effect [1][2]. Bit 7 set makes the command a DMA command,
which loads the transfer counter from the count registers on issue [1].

Bits 6–4 select a mode group — miscellaneous, disconnected, target or initiator. A command
from a group other than the chip's current mode is ignored, the command register is cleared,
and an **illegal command** interrupt is raised; after hardware or software reset the chip sits
in the disconnected state, so initiator or target commands issued too early are illegal [1].
The full opcode table is §3.2.

The sequencer keeps a working copy of the bottom of the command queue; it is flushed — so the
next stacked command falls through — by hardware reset, software reset, SCSI bus reset, bus
disconnect, bus-initiated selection or reselection, a select command, a reconnect command
with ATN set, a selection timeout, target terminate, a target-mode parity error, ATN asserted
in target mode, any initiator-mode phase change, or an illegal command [1].

#### Status / Destination bus ID ($4)

| Bit | Read (Status) | Write (Destination ID) |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | INT — mirrors the interrupt output | reserved |
| 6 | Gross Error (AMD: IOE, Illegal Operation Error) | reserved |
| 5 | Parity Error | reserved |
| 4 | Terminal Count | reserved |
| 3 | Valid Group Code (AMD: GCV) | reserved |
| 2–0 | SCSI phase bits | destination bus ID (low 3 bits) |

- **INT (7)** mirrors the INT output pin so wired-OR interrupt designs can tell whether *this*
  chip is interrupting; cleared by hardware/software reset or an interrupt-register read [1][2].
- **Gross Error (6)** is set when the top of the FIFO or the command register is overwritten,
  when the DMA and SCSI transfer directions oppose, or on an unexpected phase change in
  initiator synchronous data phase. It never interrupts by itself; it is cleared by an
  interrupt-register read while INT is asserted, or by hardware or software reset (not by a
  SCSI bus reset) [1]. The AMD part enumerates the same four conditions under the name IOE [2].
- **Parity Error (5)** is set when parity checking is enabled and an incoming byte is bad;
  in initiator role the chip also asserts ATN before releasing ACK. No interrupt by itself [1][2].
- **Terminal Count (4)** is set when the counter reaches zero and **is cleared only when the
  counter is reloaded** — by a DMA command including the DMA NOP, or by hardware or software
  reset. An interrupt-register read does *not* clear it, and a non-DMA NOP does not either [1][2].
- **Valid Group Code (3)** is set when the group-code field of the first CDB byte decodes to a
  group defined in ANSI X3.131-1986 (with the Config 2 SCSI-2 bit set, group 2 counts as a
  defined 10-byte command). Reserved groups cause a 6-byte request with the bit clear [1].
- **Phase bits (2–0)** are the live MSG/C-D/I/O phase lines: 000 Data Out, 001 Data In,
  010 Command, 011 Status, 110 Message Out, 111 Message In [1]. They are live indicators
  unless the Config 2 phase-latch bit freezes them at command completion for stacked-command
  software [1][2].
- **Destination ID (write)** takes the binary-encoded target ID for selection/reselection
  commands in its low 3 bits (111 = ID 7, which appears as $80 on the SCSI bus); the upper
  five bits are reserved. The register is unchanged by any reset [1].

#### Interrupt / Select-Reselect Time-Out ($5)

Read — the interrupt register; write — the selection/reselection time-out value:

| Bit | Read (Interrupt) |
|---|---|
| 7 | SCSI Reset Detected |
| 6 | Illegal Command |
| 5 | Disconnected |
| 4 | Bus Service |
| 3 | Function Complete |
| 2 | Reselected |
| 1 | Selected with ATN |
| 0 | Selected |

Reading this register while the interrupt output is asserted **clears the interrupt,
sequence-step and latched status registers and releases the INT pin** [1][2] — the
clear-on-read protocol that drives the whole interrupt discipline of §3.3. An interrupt
with no cause bits set is a contradiction the Mac firmware treats as fatal (§4.5) [5].

The time-out value written to the same address governs how long the chip waits for a response
after winning arbitration during selection or reselection (the chip has no way to time out of
arbitration itself — it retries until it wins). The value follows

```
RV = (time-out period × CLK frequency) / (8192 × clock conversion factor)
```

with the manual's worked example: 250 ms at 25 MHz, CCF 5, gives 153 ($99) [1]; the AMD sheet
prints the same formula and constant [2]. 250 ms is the ANSI-recommended selection abort time
[1][10]. Apple deliberately loads shorter values ("tailored … speeds to about 200mS instead of
250mS to see if anybody [complains]") computed with 7682 in place of 8192 [5]; see §4.3.

#### Sequence Step / Synchronous Transfer Period ($6)

The low three bits of the read side are the **sequence step** counter, zeroed at the start of
certain commands and incremented at fixed points of each algorithm so failed commands can be
diagnosed [1]; the per-command decode tables are in §3.2. Bit 3 is an active-low flag meaning
the synchronous offset counter has reached its maximum [1]. On the AMD part the register is
named Internal State and **bits 7:4 are reserved and undefined on reads** [2] — a detail that
matters to one Curio erratum (§5).

The write side programs the synchronous transfer period: the 5-bit value N gives **N clocks per
byte**, minimum 5 (value 4 also gives 5); values 25–31 wrap to 32–35 through zero. The value
defaults to 5 after hardware or software reset [1][2]. The 53CF96-2's FASTCLK mode changes the
clocks-per-byte mapping for fast operation [3]. The upper three bits are reserved on NCR and
AMD silicon alike [1][2].

#### FIFO Flags / Synchronous Offset ($7)

Read: the low five bits are the FIFO byte count; the upper three bits duplicate the sequence
step. The flags "should not be polled while transferring data because they will not be stable
while the SCSI interface is changing the contents of the FIFO" [1] — the Mac driver only reads
them after an interrupt, for residual accounting (§4.7) [5]. In test mode bit 5 instead
indicates the synchronous offset counter is non-zero [1].

Write: the low four bits are the **synchronous offset** — 0 means asynchronous, any other
value the number of bytes that may be outstanding without a handshake. Maximum offset is 15 in
normal operation, **7 when Threshold-8/burst mode is in use** [1][2]; the CF96-2 adds REQ/ACK
assertion and deassertion delay fields in bits 7–4, which are reserved on the plain and AMD
parts [2][3]. The offset clears to zero on hardware or software reset [1].

#### Configuration 1 ($8)

| Bit | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | Slow Cable (AMD: ETM, Extended Timing Mode) | inserts an extra clock between data assertion and REQ/ACK — compensates capacitive loading [1][2] |
| 6 | SCSI Reset Reporting Interrupt Disable | suppresses the SCSI-reset interrupt; the chip still disconnects and idles [1] |
| 5 | Parity Test Mode | forces the parity bit to equal bit 7 — for testing only [1] |
| 4 | Enable Parity Checking | check parity on incoming SCSI bytes; initiator-side errors also assert ATN [1][2] |
| 3 | Chip Test Mode Enable | enables the Test register; a hard or soft reset is required to leave it [1] |
| 2–0 | My Bus ID | this device's bus ID, for responding to selection/reselection and for arbitration [1] |

The register reads back as written [1]. The AMD part specifies that the **own-ID field is not
affected by any reset** — undefined at power-up, retained thereafter [2]; Apple's driver relies
on exactly that when it re-reads the ID out of Config 1 during reselection processing (§4.9) [5].

#### Clock Conversion Factor ($9, write-only)

Must be programmed to agree with the CLK input; "all timings longer than 400 ns depend on
this register correctly agreeing with the CLK frequency" [1]:

| CLK (MHz) | Factor |
|---|---|
| 10 | 2 |
| 10.01–15 | 3 |
| 15.01–20 | 4 |
| 20.01–25 | 5 |
| 25.01–30 | 6 |
| 30.01–35 | 7 |
| 35.01–40 | **0 (encodes 8)** — 53CF96-2 only [3] |

The value **1 is forbidden**. Hardware or software reset sets the register to 2; SCSI bus reset
does not affect it. The upper five bits are reserved [1][2][3]. Apple's equates define the full
ladder including the 0-means-8 entry, which only the 8100's 53CF96 uses [5].

#### Test ($A, write-only)

Reachable only after Config 1 bit 3 opens it, and a hard or soft reset must follow before
normal operation [1]. Bit 2 floats all outputs; bit 1 forces initiator mode; bit 0 forces
target mode — the mode bits exist so commands can be exercised without a bus [1]. The Mac
firmware never touches it.

#### Configuration 2 ($B)

After hardware or software reset this register reads $00, which makes the chip
software-compatible with the older 53C90; any pattern written may be read back identically [1].

| Bit | NCR 53C94/95/96 name | AMD Am53C94/96 name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | Reserve FIFO Byte | DAE, Data Alignment Enable | reserves one byte in the FIFO bottom for misaligned synchronous data in, staged via register $F (§3.4) [1][2] |
| 6 | Enable Phase Latch (EPL); on the 53CF96-2, **Features Enable (FE)** | LSP, Latch SCSI Phase | plain part: latch the status phase bits at command completion; the CF96-2's FE additionally enables the 24-bit counter (register $E) and the chip-ID readout [1][2][3] |
| 5 | Enable Byte Control | (mode 2 only) | lets BHE/SA0 steer the DMA byte lanes [1] |
| 4 | DREQ Hi-Z | — | floats DREQ and makes the chip ignore DACK/ [1] |
| 3 | SCSI-2 | — | 3-byte tagged-queue messages and 10-byte group-2 CDBs [1] |
| 2 | Target Bad Parity Abort | — | abort a target receive command on bad parity [1] |
| 1 | Register Parity Enable | — | take FIFO parity from the host DBP pin on CS/ writes [1] |
| 0 | DMA Parity Enable | — | take FIFO parity from the host DBP pins on DACK/ writes [1] |

The AMD part's bit 6 latches the phase bits and nothing more — there is no 24-bit counter and
no chip ID on that silicon [2]. Apple's comment trail records the same reading: "disable phase
latch (Ie. status regr is a live state indicator of SCSI phase lines); for f96 part, this bit
turns on 24bit xfer count as well" [5].

#### Configuration 3 ($C)

Cleared by hardware or software reset (53C90 software compatibility again), reads back as
written [1].

| Bit | Plain/AMD name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Save Residual Byte (AMD: LBTM, Last Byte Transfer Mode) | suppress DREQ for a lone trailing byte of a 16-bit DMA receive; the byte stays in the FIFO for the processor (§3.4). No effect in 8-bit DMA [1][2] |
| 1 | Alternate DMA (AMD: MDM, Modified DMA Mode) | with Threshold-8, converts the DMA interface to 4-word bursts — the 8237 demand-mode handshake (§3.4) [1][2] |
| 0 | Threshold 8 (AMD: BS8, Burst Size 8) | withhold DREQ until eight bytes can move; caps the synchronous offset at 7 [1][2] |

On the AMD part **bits 7–3 are reserved** [2]. The 53CF96-2 defines them [3]:

| Bit | 53CF96-2 name |
|---|---|
| 7 | ID-Message Reserved Check |
| 6 | Queue Tag Enable |
| 5 | CDB10 |
| 4 | **FASTSCSI** |
| 3 | **FASTCLK** |

With FASTCLK clear the chip does 2 clocks per byte asynchronous and 5 per byte synchronous
(5 MB/s at 25 MHz); with FASTCLK and FASTSCSI both set it reaches 10 MB/s at 40 MHz, the fast
synchronous period register range being values 4–7 [3]. Apple's driver flips FASTSCSI per
negotiated period (§4.10) [5].

#### Configuration 4 ($D, 53CF96-2 only)

Present only on the fast part: bit 2 active negation (required for fast operation), bit 1
transfer-counter test, bit 0 back-to-back transfer enable; bits 7–3 read zero [3].

#### Counter High / Chip ID ($E, 53CF96-2 only)

With Features Enable set, the third counter byte (write) and — the part's signature — a read
that returns the **chip ID $A2** [3]. A plain 53C94-class part has no register here at all;
on the AMD cell the address is undecoded. Apple's probe reads exactly this register after a
Reset/NOP/FE/DMA-NOP sequence and tests for $A2 (§4.4) [5].

#### FIFO Bottom / Data Alignment ($F, write-only)

The staging byte for the reserve-FIFO-byte feature: with Config 2 bit 7 set before a phase
change to initiator synchronous data in, the microprocessor writes the odd low byte here after
the interrupt, and the chip assembles the first 16-bit DMA word from it plus the first received
byte [1]. NCR names the register FIFO Bottom; the AMD part implements the same role as the
**Data Alignment Register (DALREG)**, enabled by its DAE bit [1][2]. Writing the register
after such an interrupt also clears the Config 2 reserve bit [1]. The transfer count must be
**overprogrammed by one** to account for the reserved byte [1].

#### The pseudo-DMA aperture (+$100)

Not a chip register but a platform decode: an access to `base + $100` performs a FIFO data
cycle on the chip as though the DMA controller were doing it, while a DMA-variant command is
open. The Mac's "TurboSCSI" data path is built on it — §3.8.

### 2.4 Reset values

Three reset classes exist (§3.6); per-register survival across them [1][2][3]:

reset routine issues an explicit Flush FIFO afterwards (§4.11) [5].

## 3. Behaviour

### 3.1 Chip modes and command stacking

The chip is always in one of three sequencer modes — **disconnected**, **initiator** or
**target**. A select command puts it in initiator mode, a reselect sequence command in target
mode, and being selected/reselected on the bus in whichever role the other device chose;
Enable Selection/Reselection by itself changes nothing until an event arrives [1]. Command
opcodes come in four mode groups (§2.3); cross-group commands are illegal.

Two commands may be stacked in the command FIFO, producing two interrupts; when the first
interrupt is serviced, the status, sequence-step and interrupt registers change to describe
the second. Stacked-command software should set the phase latch (Config 2 bit 6) so the phase
bits survive [1] — Apple's driver does not stack commands and instead keeps the phase bits
live [5].

### 3.2 Arbitration and the connection sequences

**Initiated selection.** A select command makes the chip wait for bus free, arbitrate —
retrying indefinitely until it wins — and then run the selection, message-out and
command-out phases autonomously, interrupting when done. Issuing any selection command
**cancels Enable Selection/Reselection once arbitration is won**; ANSI recommends re-enabling
within 250 ms so other devices' attempts do not time out [1]. If a bus-initiated event wins
the race instead, the FIFO and command register are cleared and further microprocessor writes
are ignored until the interrupt register is read — any message bytes staged for the select
are lost [1].

The initiator select commands and their sequence-step decode tables [1] Tables 8, 9, 10, 11:

*Select without ATN ($41/$C1)* — arbitrates, selects, sends the 6/10/12-byte CDB from the FIFO
or by DMA (transfer count 6/10/12):

| Seq step | Interrupt | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Disconnected ($20) | Arbitration complete; selection time-out; disconnected |
| 2 | Bus Service + Function Complete ($18) | Selection complete; target did not assert Command phase |
| 3 | $18 | Stopped during command transfer; target changed phase early |
| 4 | $18 | Sequence complete |

*Select with ATN ($42/$C2)* — selects with ATN true, sends one message byte then the CDB
(transfer count 7/11/13 for DMA):

| Seq step | Interrupt | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Disconnected | Selection time-out; disconnected |
| 0 | $18 | Selection complete; target did not assert Message Out; ATN still driven |

*Select with ATN and Stop ($43/$C3)* — for multi-byte message-outs: selects with ATN, sends

| Seq step | Interrupt | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Disconnected | Selection time-out |
| 0 | $18 | Selection complete; target did not assert Message Out; ATN still asserted |

*Select with ATN3 ($46/$C6)* — as Select with ATN but three message bytes (the SCSI-2
tagged-queue case), then the CDB.

**Bus-initiated selection** (the chip selected as target, after Enable Selection/Reselection):
the FIFO ends up holding the raw bus-ID byte (one byte, un-encoded — every data bit true during
selection is set, so both IDs appear), then the identify message (or a null byte if selected
without ATN), then the CDB — which begins at the third or fifth FIFO byte. With the SCSI-2 bit
set and ATN held, the chip may take two more message bytes for a queue tag [1].

**Bus-initiated reselection** (the chip reselected as initiator): the FIFO holds the bus-ID
byte and the identify message, plus two queue-tag bytes if tagged queuing is enabled [1].
Apple's driver peels the ID byte, masks off its own ID bit — which it reads back out of Config 1
— and priority-decodes the remainder to identify the reselecting target (§4.9) [5].

### 3.3 Interrupts

The INT output is active-low, open-drain, latched on the rising edge of CLK. It is cleared by
reading the interrupt register, by hardware reset, or by software reset chip — **not by a
SCSI bus reset** — and cannot be masked [1][2]. The service protocol is fixed by the
clear-on-read rule: read Sequence Step, Status and FIFO Flags first (they are side-effect
free), read the Interrupt register **last**, which clears all three and drops INT [1][5].

If RST pulses on the bus, the chip disconnects and resets its sequencer; a SCSI Reset Detected
interrupt is raised unless suppressed by Config 1 bit 6. If the bus reset is still active when
the interrupt is cleared, a **new interrupt is generated** and must be serviced too [1].

**Platform wiring.** The chip's INT and DREQ pins go only to the platform's I/O chip; the
Macintosh never wires them to the CPU directly.

On the PDM machines both lines terminate on the **AMIC**, which mirrors them as *live level*
bits in its pseudo-VIA2 "device bank" [4] Figure 2-2 p. 23, [8] sheet 12:

| Register | Address | Bits (7→0) |
|---|---|---|
| Interrupt flag (IFR, read) | $50F2 6003 | VIA2 IRQ \| SCSI-B IRQ \| FDC IRQ \| 0 \| SCSI-A IRQ \| SCSI-B DRQ \| ANY SLOT \| SCSI-A DRQ |
| Interrupt enable (IER, r/w) | $50F2 6013 | SET/CLR \| SCSI-B ENA \| FDC ENA \| 0 \| SCSI-A ENA \| SCSI-B DRQ ENA \| ANY SLOT \| SCSI-A DRQ ENA |

SCSI-A is the Curio (IRQ bit 3, DRQ bit 0); SCSI-B is the 8100's 53CF96 (IRQ bit 6, DRQ bit 2),
absent on the 6100/7100 [4] Figure 2-2, [5]. The IFR bits are **level indicators of the chip
pins, not latches** — Apple's HAL registers the interrupt as LEVEL-sensitive ("no clear
necessary") and skips its clear primitive for it; the interrupt deasserts when the chip's
interrupt register is read [5]. The IER uses the classic VIA convention (bit 7 set writes the
1-bits, bit 7 clear clears them); the DRQ bits are polled only and their enable bits are never
set [5]. Enabled IFR bits OR into bit 7, which raises the emulated level-2 interrupt; the Mac's
level-2 dispatch table slots 3 and 6 carry the SCSI ISRs [5]. The bank partial-decodes its
low address bits, so the same registers also answer at the classic VIA stride — a platform
quirk documented with the AMIC itself (see
[AMIC](../../machines/pdm/amic.md)) [5][7].

On the Quadra generation the wiring differs per platform [5]: the DAFB machines expose the
SCSI interrupt as an **edge-sensitive** VIA2 bit at $50F03A03 (enable at $50F03C13) and the
DREQ line as a bit in a **DAFB register** (§4.2); the two-bus Quadras share one VIA bit.

### 3.4 The DMA engine

The DMA interface runs asynchronously to the chip clock. **DREQ** is asserted (tri-state,
active-high) whenever the FIFO can move data, and is released on the leading edge of **DACK**/;
DREQ is never asserted while DACK/ is active [1]. DACK/ accesses the FIFO only — the address
pins are ignored — while CS/ can reach the FIFO or any register; in single-bus strap modes the
two must never be active simultaneously [1][2].

**Threshold.** DREQ asserts when the FIFO crosses the service threshold: one byte in 8-bit
DMA, one word in 16-bit DMA. The counter decrements by the number of bytes moved regardless
of transfer size [1].

**Direction rules** [1]:

- *DMA read* (FIFO to memory): DREQ true while the FIFO holds at least the threshold;
  deasserts when the FIFO empties, or when the counter reaches zero and the FIFO is empty.
  Total bytes moved = FIFO content at start + programmed count.
- *DMA write* (memory to FIFO): 8-bit — DREQ true while one element is free, deasserts at 16
  bytes or terminal count; 16-bit — true while two elements are free, deasserts at 15–16
  bytes or terminal count.

**Threshold-8 mode** (Config 3 bit 0) raises the threshold to eight bytes in data phases
only, so the DMA bus can run five to ten times faster than the SCSI device and the SCSI
operation proceeds in parallel with other bus activity. With it set, the synchronous offset
is capped at 7 [1][2]. Near the end of a transfer — counter below eight — the chip drops back
to the normal threshold so the last bytes complete [1].

**Burst / Alternate DMA mode** (Config 3 bit 1, meaningful only with Threshold-8) retimes the
interface for an 8237-class controller: DREQ is deasserted after the third word of a burst, so
the controller relinquishes the bus after exactly four words (eight bytes), letting DRAM
refresh or another master arbitrate. The last burst is one to four words; a one-transfer
burst follows non-burst timing. DACK/ then stays asserted across the burst while the read or
write strobe toggles per transfer (mode-dependent) [1][2]. In AMD nomenclature these are BS8
and MDM, and "both bits have to be set for proper operation" of burst DMA [2]; the pair
allows offset 15 again, though Apple caps at 7 regardless (§4.10) [2][5].

**Save Residual Byte** (Config 3 bit 2) handles the modulo-2 remainder of a 16-bit DMA
receive: with it set, DREQ is *not* asserted for a lone trailing byte; the byte stays in the
FIFO and the completion interrupt fires with the FIFO count at 1, the processor reading the
byte out through the FIFO register. With it clear, the last byte goes out as a 16-bit cycle
with the upper half driven to ones. The bit is operational only in 16-bit DMA receive, is
ignored in data-out, and should not be combined with byte control [1][2]. Apple's DMA engine
enables it and reads the odd byte from the FIFO on completion (§4.7) [5].

**Misaligned boundaries.** For 16-bit DMA into odd memory addresses the chip provides
"hooks": the processor preloads the FIFO with the low byte of the first word (FIFO preloading
before a command, for data-out; the FIFO Bottom register $F after the phase-change interrupt,
for initiator synchronous data-in with Config 2 bit 7 set); the first DMA word then overwrites
that low byte with a copy of itself. The transfer count must be overprogrammed by one for the
reserved byte, and the reserve-FIFO-byte feature is only usable in synchronous data-in [1].

### 3.5 Timing

- **Clock.** 10–25 MHz on the 53C94/95/96 and AMD parts (12 MHz minimum for synchronous
  operation) [1][2]; 36–40 MHz on the 53CF96-2 via the 0-encodes-8 clock factor [3].
- **Transfer rate.** Synchronous period = register value × CLK period, sustained 5 MB/s over
  commercial conditions; the fast part reaches 10 MB/s at 40 MHz with FASTCLK+FASTSCSI [1][3].
  Asynchronous rate depends on cable length and CLK: up to 6 MB/s on one-foot cables with
  typical devices, typically 4 MB/s over six meters, worst case 3 MB/s at maximum
  single-ended cable length; receiving rate is barely clock-sensitive [1].
- **Selection time-out.** RV = t × CLK / (8192 × CCF), per §2.3; the chip keeps arbitrating
  until it wins, then times the response [1][2].
- **RST assertion.** The Reset SCSI Bus command drives RSTO/ for T2 = 130 × CLK period × CCF
  — about 26 µs at 20 MHz/CCF 4 — and returns the chip to the disconnected state with no
  command-complete interrupt (though RSTI is externally tied to RSTO, so a SCSI-reset
  interrupt follows anyway unless masked) [1]. The AMD sheet prints the same figure with an
  internal unit inconsistency ("approximately 25 ms" in one place, "25 µs" in another); the
  NCR formula is the self-consistent one, and it matches ANSI's 25 µs minimum [2][10] (*the
  µs/ms reconciliation is inferred*).
- **Disconnect latency.** If BSY drops while the chip is connected as an initiator, the
  disconnect interrupt fires 1.5 to 3.5 CLK cycles later [1].

### 3.6 Reset classes

Three reset levels [1] Ch. 2 "Chip Reset":

- **Hard reset** — the RESET pin (asserted at least two CLK periods) or the Reset Chip
  command. Stops everything, resets all functions, returns to the disconnected state. The
  reset command stays at the top of the command FIFO, **locking the chip and its registers
  until a NOP is written** [1][2].
- **Soft reset** — a SCSI bus reset received on RSTI/, or issued through the Reset SCSI Bus
  command. Resets the DMA interface, the bus-initiated selection/reselection module, the
  command sequencer, the sequence step and the sequencer-mode bits; empties the command FIFO;
  releases all SCSI signals except RSTO/. **Does not clear the configuration registers, the
  sync period, the latched status error bits or a pending interrupt** [1][2].
- **Disconnect reset** — issued whenever the chip disconnects (target disconnect/terminate
  commands, bus free in initiator mode, selection timeout). A subset of the soft reset: clears
  the sequencer-mode bits, empties the command FIFO, releases the bus signals [1].

### 3.7 Parity

Six control bits govern parity generation and checking [1] Table 2-1: Config 1 bit 4 enables
parity checking on SCSI-to-FIFO bytes (bad parity sets the status bit and, in initiator role,
asserts ATN — never an interrupt by itself); Config 1 bit 5 forces the parity bit to replicate
bit 7 for testing; Config 2 bits 0–1 select whether the host DBP parity pins feed the FIFO on
DMA and register writes or the chip generates parity; the Target Bad Parity Abort bit stops a
target receive on bad parity, leaving the counter and FIFO flags as the record of what
transferred [1]. In dual-bus modes the processor sees no FIFO parity and the internal
generator supplies it [1]. The parity bit always travels through the FIFO with its byte [1].

### 3.8 Pseudo-DMA — the "TurboSCSI" aperture

The chip can be driven at full speed without any DMA controller at all: a **CPU access to a
platform-decoded aperture** (`base + $100` on the Mac) is presented to the chip as a FIFO
data cycle, so a `move.w` loop through the aperture moves two FIFO bytes per iteration while a
DMA-variant command (with its counter loaded and DREQ semantics in force) runs the SCSI side.
Apple's firmware calls the register `rDMA` and the technique, on the Quadra generation,
"Turbo SCSI" [5][6].

Two eras of the wrapper exist, both visible in Apple's platform descriptors [5]:

- **Quadra generation (DAFB machines, `kRealQuadra`)**: the aperture has a second,
  **non-serialized alias at `+$40000`** — the same chip image reached through a different page
  so the transfer loop's accesses do not serialize against the interrupt registers — and the
  DREQ line is read back from a **DAFB frame-buffer controller register**, with the address
  marked as needing an MMU page swap in software. There is no true DMA; the descriptor states
  `HBAhasDMA = false`, `HBAhasPseudoDMA = true`. The DAFB SCSI control register at $F9800024
  must be initialized by the driver with a value selected by CPU clock speed before transfers
  work (§4.2) [5][6].
- **PDM (`kPDM`)**: the aperture is at `base + $100` with **no non-serialized alias**
  (`pdmaNonSerlzdAddr = pdmaAddr`), DREQ is read from the AMIC pseudo-VIA2 DRQ bits (§3.3),
  and **true AMIC DMA exists alongside** the aperture (§4.7) [5]. Early PDM prototypes
  (EVT1 boards, behind a `forPDMProto` build flag) had pseudo-DMA without the handshaked
  variant [5].

The aperture is **handshaked**: an access issued when the chip has no data (or no FIFO space)
does not complete blindly. Apple's SCSI Manager installs a **bus-error handler** for exactly
this case, checks the fault address against its pseudo-DMA addresses, and resumes its
transfer loop on phase change — the firmware expects and recovers from these faults during
blind transfers [5][6]. The underlying termination mechanism on PDM is the AMIC's 40 µs
undecoded-address error behavior [4] pp. 21–22 (*inferred — unverified*: the stall-and-fault
model is consistent with the handler and with the documented error timing, but no document
spells out the aperture's internal stall logic). 16-bit accesses move two FIFO bytes, first
byte in the high half [5] (*observed* in the driver's transfer loops; the exact lane
arrangement inside AMIC is outside this page's evidence).

## 4. Programming model

### 4.1 The software stack

From the Quadra 900 ROM (1991) onward Apple drives the 53C96-class chips with a layered SCSI
Manager [5][6]:

- **SCSI Manager 96** (Quadra generation): a monolithic driver with per-platform hardware
  sections ("TERROR"-vintage sources, 1991) [6].
- **SCSI Manager 4.3** (AV Quadras and PDM): an XPT/SIM architecture — a cross-platform
  transporter, a motherboard SCSI interface module, and per-platform **hardware abstraction
  layers (HAL)**. The 53C96-family HAL (`HALc96*`) is one code body parameterized by a
  per-bus hardware descriptor: register base, pseudo-DMA address, interrupt/DREQ register
  addresses and bit numbers, interrupt sense, DMA type and alignment, and feature flags [5].
  The old SCSI Manager API (`SCSIGet`/`SCSISelect`/`SCSIRead`/…) is translated onto the same
  SIM and HAL, so **all SCSI traffic, boot included, goes through the register usage
  described here** [5].

The PDM descriptor values, verbatim from the HAL initialization code [5]:

| pdmaAddr | base + $100 (no non-serialized alias) | base + $100 |
| dreqAddr / intFlagSCSIAddr | $50F2 6003 | $50F2 6003 |

### 4.2 Quadra-generation firmware: the DAFB "Turbo SCSI" driver

The Quadra-generation driver initializes the DAFB's Turbo SCSI channel before driving the
chip: at SCSI Manager init it writes a state-machine configuration value into DAFB register
$F9800024 — $1EC on 25 MHz-CPU machines, $041 on 33 MHz-CPU machines — selected from the CPU
clock speed, and it dynamically selects the chip's clock conversion factor and selection
timeout the same way [5][6]. Its transfer engine is a set of "Fast" and "Slow" register loops
(`FastRead_96`, `FastWrite_96`, `SlowRead_96`, `SlowComp_96`, …) that move data through the
aperture, wait on DREQ-or-interrupt, and run under a permanent bus-error handler; the
non-serialized register image (`pdmaAddr + $40000`) is the data path for the fast loops, with
interleaved NOPs added "to squish pipeline" at high CPU speeds [5][6]. The driver's change
history notes residual-byte and phase-change race fixes in the fast loops — evidence that the
aperture's handshaked semantics and the chip's FIFO/residual rules (§3.4, §5) were load-bearing
from the start [6]. The ROM's power-on self-test runs a "53C96 Turbo SCSI chip" test [6].

The 53C96's own register programming on these machines follows the same chip-level sequences
as below; only the wrapper differs. The DAFB register semantics beyond what the driver writes
are outside this page's evidence (§6).

### 4.3 PDM hardware initialization sequence

At SCSI Manager init the ROM runs the 53C96-family hardware-init for each bus, in order
(*observed* in the HAL source [5]):

1. Mask the SCSI interrupt in the AMIC enable register; run the CF96-2 probe (§4.4).
2. Write **Reset Chip** ($02), then **NOP** ($00) — mandatory after any hard or software
   reset — then **Flush FIFO** ($01).
3. Write **Config 1 = initiator ID | $40** — the Mac's own bus ID plus the SCSI-reset-report
   interrupt disable. The ID comes from parameter RAM byte 2, low 3 bits, normally 7.
   Parity checking is left **off** (bit 4 clear).
4. Write **Config 2 = $08** — SCSI-2 mode only; Features Enable stays off (phase bits live,
   16-bit counter).
5. Write **Config 3 = $04** — Save Residual Byte only at this point; Threshold-8 and
   Alternate DMA are added later around each DMA data phase.
6. Write the **clock conversion factor** and **selection timeout** per bus: the Curio at
   20 MHz gets CCF 4 and time-out value 132 ($84); the 53CF96 at 40 MHz gets CCF 0 (encoding
   8) and the same 132. Both work out to ≈ 216 ms by the manuals' formula, or ≈ 203 ms by the
   7682 constant in Apple's own comments — a deliberate tightening of ANSI's 250 ms
   recommendation (§3.5) [1][2][5].
7. Write **Synchronous Offset = 0** (asynchronous).
8. Read the **Interrupt register twice** — a throwaway read to clear any latched status, and a
   second in case an interrupt was stacked behind the first. Because the PDM interrupt is
   level-sensitive, no VIA-side clear is performed.
9. Clear the AMIC DMA channel's RUN bit (§4.7).

The software half of init then fixes the two Config 3 working values — normal $04, DMA $07
(Save Residual + Threshold-8 + Alternate DMA) — which the HAL flips between around every DMA
data phase, and allocates a page-aligned, locked 4 KB copy buffer for double-buffered DMA,
with a $200-byte minimum useful DMA size [5].

### 4.4 The fast-SCSI (53CF96-2) probe

Before each bus is configured, the HAL runs the identification sequence [3][5]:

```
Reset Chip ($02) → NOP ($00) → Config 2 = $40 (Features Enable)
→ DMA NOP ($80) → read register $E0
```

$A2 identifies a 53CF96-2; anything else is the plain core. On the Curio cell the sequence is
guaranteed harmless: Config 2 $40 merely sets the AMD part's phase-latch bit, the DMA NOP
just reloads the counter, and there is no register at $E at all, so the read returns undefined
data that cannot equal $A2 [2][5]. A positive probe sets the `HBAisFast` flag, which selects
the 40 MHz clock factor, the fast bus's offered sync parameters (§4.10) and the SCSI-B
interrupt bits [5].

### 4.5 Interrupt service protocol

On every interrupt (or poll) the HAL reads the three side-effect-free registers first and
**the interrupt register last**: Sequence Step, Status, FIFO Flags, then Interrupt — the read
of the last clearing all three and dropping the INT pin [5]. The chip contract this pins
down: sequence step, status and FIFO flags must be readable at any time without side effects,
and a read of the interrupt register *while INT is asserted* is the only thing that clears
the latches [1][5]. If the interrupt register reads zero in this path the HAL panics
("no c9x interrupt cause bits set") — a spurious INT level is a fatal inconsistency, not an
event [5]. Any interrupt containing Illegal Command, Reselected, Selected, Selected-with-ATN or
SCSI-Reset-Detected takes the reselection/selection path (§4.9); everything else is a normal
data/phase event [5].

The HAL also **polls Status bit 7** with interrupts blocked in its per-byte transfer loops,
so the INT mirror in Status must be live and independent of the VIA-side enable [5].

### 4.6 Selection

Two selection paths exist, both writing the Destination ID register first [5]:

- **New API (`DoInitiate`)**: the common case stages the IDENTIFY message byte and the CDB
  into the FIFO and issues **Select with ATN** ($42); a queue-tagged command stages three
  message bytes and issues **Select with ATN3** ($46); other multi-byte message-outs use
  **Select with ATN and Stop** ($43), then feed the remaining message bytes and the CDB with
  Transfer Information ($10) in Message Out and Command phase. When the client disables
  select-with-ATN (the boot path does — §4.12), the CDB alone goes into the FIFO and
  **Select without ATN** ($41) is issued. Success is an interrupt with the Interrupt register
  reading $18 (Bus Service + Function Complete); leftover FIFO bytes distinguish the
  partial-message/partial-command failure cases; a Disconnected interrupt means selection
  timeout; a Reselected/Selected interrupt means the chip lost the race and the §4.9 path
  runs instead [5].
- **Old API (`DoSelect`, the TIB-style calls)**: the transfer count is loaded with 2 and a
  **DMA select** ($C1 without ATN, $C2 with) is issued. The chip runs arbitration and
  selection, then **asserts DREQ asking for the message/command bytes via DMA** — the
  firmware parks in a three-way wait (DREQ polled at $50F2 6003 / chip interrupt / ~4 s
  software watchdog) and the client's subsequent calls feed the bytes through the
  pseudo-DMA aperture while the select command is still open. The state machine
  (NeedMsgOut → NeedCmdSent → FCIntPend) tracks how far the paused select has progressed [5].

### 4.7 Data phases — true DMA, PIO, and residuals

For big transfers the PDM HAL drives the **AMIC SCSI DMA channel**, a single-shot,
address-only channel per controller: a 32-bit physical buffer address register (byte-writable,
MSB at +0) and a one-byte control register with RUN (bit 1), FLUSH (bit 4, self-clearing),
DIR (bit 6, set = memory-to-SCSI) and RST (bit 0), plus interrupt bits the SCSI driver never
uses — there is **no byte count register and no completion interrupt**; the 53C96's own
transfer counter terminates the transfer, and the chip's interrupt is the only completion
signal [5]. Full channel semantics live with the AMIC page
([AMIC](../../machines/pdm/amic.md)).

Per chunk (*observed* in the DMA engine [5]):

1. Align the user buffer to 8 bytes with per-byte Transfer Information ($10) loops, and chop
   the transfer so only multiples of 8 go through DMA; a tail under 8 bytes is finished
   per-byte afterwards.
2. Set **Config 3 = $07** (Threshold-8 + Alternate DMA + Save Residual) for the duration.
3. Choose the chunk: a whole physically contiguous run of the locked user buffer up to 64 KB
   is DMA'd directly; buffers in ROM/NuBus space, non-lockable pages and transfers at or
   below the $200-byte minimum go through the 4 KB copy buffer instead.
4. Program the count registers (≤ $FFFF; 0 = 64 K) and issue **DMA Transfer Information**
   ($90).
5. Write the physical address into the channel, MSB first; clear DIR for reads, set DIR for
   writes; set RUN.
6. Sleep until the chip interrupts — terminal count with REQ pending for the next phase, or a
   phase change, or a disconnect.
7. Stop the channel: clear RUN; for reads also set FLUSH and **poll until it self-clears**
   (the hardware writes any partial buffered doubleword out to memory first). There is no
   wait-for-completion call at all for this platform — the routine is empty [5].
8. Account residuals from the chip: remaining = (counter mid << 8) | counter low;
   transferred = chunk − remaining. On a read, a FIFO count of exactly 1 is the odd trailing
   byte — read it from the FIFO into the buffer (Save Residual semantics; DREQ was never
   asserted for it); more than 1 leftover byte on a read is a system error. On a write, any
   FIFO leftovers are subtracted from the count and **Flush FIFO** is issued.
9. Loop while the live Status phase bits still say data phase; finish per-byte under 15 bytes
   remaining; on disconnect, record the interrupt snapshot and unwind with the residual.
10. Restore **Config 3 = $04** on every exit path [5].

Small transfers, odd remnants and the "slow" vectors move data per-byte through the FIFO
register — $10, poll Status bit 7, read the registers, read the FIFO (in) — or through the
**pseudo-DMA aperture** as 4 × `move.w` per DREQ poll, with the phase re-checked from the live
Status bits between iterations [5]. The aperture loops are also the fallback the bus-error
handler resumes (§3.8) [5].

A disconnect mid-transfer requires nothing from the DMA engine beyond RUN cleared (and FLUSH
completed on reads): the chip raises its interrupt per its own rules, the counter holds the
residual, the FIFO holds what was never handed over, and no AMIC state is lost or reported [5].

### 4.8 Status, message, and command complete

In Status phase the initiator issues **Initiator Command Complete** ($11); on Function
Complete the FIFO holds the target's status byte followed by the command-complete message
byte — the chip leaves **ACK asserted** so the target cannot disconnect while the firmware
examines them. Accepting the message means issuing **Message Accepted** ($12), which releases
ACK; rejecting it means setting ATN first ($1A) [1][5]. The firmware then waits for the
Disconnected interrupt (bus free) [5]. Message-in sequences in general run one byte per
Transfer Information, with Message Accepted between bytes and ACK held on the final byte
until the software decides [1][5]; message-out after Set ATN stages all bytes into the FIFO
and transfers them with one command — the chip drops ATN before the last byte [1][5]. A
Transfer Pad ($98) or byte-bucketing loop pushes a wedged target toward Status phase [1][5].

### 4.9 Disconnect and reselection

On every Disconnected interrupt the HAL runs a fixed epilogue: **Flush FIFO**, **Synchronous
Offset = 0**, clear the fast-SCSI bit (Config 3 bit 4 on the CF96 bus), then **Enable
Selection/Reselection** ($44) [5] — necessary every time, because the chip disables its
selection/reselection circuit automatically upon disconnecting [2]. Between jobs the chip
therefore always sits armed for reselection.

On a Reselected interrupt the FIFO holds the raw bus-ID byte (both IDs' bits set) and, unless
Bus Service is also set, the IDENTIFY message behind it; Bus Service set means the target
stopped before Message In and no message byte is present. The HAL subtracts its own ID bit —
read back out of Config 1 — and priority-decodes the remaining bit to get the reselecting
target [1][5]. A bus-initiated Selection of the Mac is fielded the same way and reported as
selected-as-target; Apple's driver defines the target-mode opcodes "so that we can respond
to a select without toasting the bus" but never runs target sequences [5].

### 4.10 Synchronous negotiation

Given an SDTR message (period factor in 4 ns units, offset), the HAL writes the synchronous
period register from a factor table, sets Config 3 FASTSCSI (bit 4, CF96 bus) if and only if
the factor is under 50 (period < 200 ns), and writes the offset — capped at **7** because
Threshold-8 is in use (the chip would allow 15 in the combined burst mode, so the cap is
conservative but valid) [1][2][5]. Offered parameters: minimum factor 25 (100 ns, 10 MB/s) on
the 8100's fast bus; 50 (200 ns, 5 MB/s) on the Curio bus; maximum offset 7 [5]. The system
initiates negotiation only when a client asks for synchronous transfer, but always answers
target-initiated SDTR [5]. The offset is re-zeroed at every disconnect; the period register
retains its value [5].

### 4.11 Bus reset

The HAL's bus reset issues **Reset SCSI Bus** ($03) — no interrupt is generated because
Config 1 bit 6 was set at init — runs two ~128 ms delay loops, polls and eats any stray
interrupt, then issues **Flush FIFO**, because the SCSI-bus soft reset does not flush the
FIFO (§2.4, §3.6), and marks the bus free [1][2][5]. The hardware asserts RST for its own
130 × CLK × CCF interval (§3.5); the settle time is owned by software [1][5].

### 4.12 Boot

Because the PDM ROM carries SCSI Manager 4.3, the Start Manager's startup-device search calls
the SCSI Manager's boot module rather than the classic SCSI loader [5]. It inquires the XPT
for the bus count and per-bus parameters, resolves the PRAM startup record (new slot format:
bus, target = upper 5 bits, LUN = low 3 bits; old format: refnum-derived ID), then — unless
told to load only the startup device — **scans every bus, target ID 7 down to 0, skipping the
initiator, LUN 0**, reading block 0 of each and loading a partition-map driver whose type
matches the PRAM driver preference byte [5]. Boot reads use **READ(6) ($08)** — READ(10) is
deliberately avoided for 21-bit addresses because of a contemporary drive erratum — polled
transfer, autosense disabled, parity off, and selection **without** ATN except in the
nonzero-LUN PRAM case; a power-on CHECK CONDITION is retried exactly once [5]. A 15-tick poll
loop re-invokes the startup-device load until the PRAM spin-up timeout expires, waiting for a
slow internal disk [5].

On a single-controller machine with default PRAM, this dance has an observable consequence:
the machine sits out the full spin-up timeout (20 s at the default PRAM setting) before
booting from the drive queue — the result of a feature-flag overwrite bug in the HAL's
bus-inquiry report that only manifests with one SCSI chip, compounded by the boot code's
default-PRAM fallback (*observed* in the ROM's behavior [5][7]). The wait is authentic
firmware behavior, not a hardware fault; §6 lists what remains unverified about it.

## 5. Quirks & errata

- **Three "53C96"s.** The AMD Am53C96 is a 53C94-class second source, not a fast part; only
  the NCR 53CF96-2 has Config 4, the 24-bit counter, the chip ID and the fast modes (§1.2).
  Reads of $D0/$E0 on the Curio cell hit undecoded space — which is precisely what makes the
  ID probe reliable.
- **Reset Chip locks the chip** until a NOP is written; every Apple init sequence does
  Reset → NOP → Flush in that order (§4.3) [1][2][5].
- **A SCSI bus reset must not flush the FIFO** or clear a pending interrupt — the chip's
  documented soft-reset scope (§3.6) — and Apple's reset routine compensates with an explicit
  Flush FIFO after polling for the reset interrupt [1][2][5].
- **Interrupt read clears three registers at once.** Sequence Step, Status and FIFO Flags
  must read side-effect-free; the Interrupt register must be read last, and only while INT is
  asserted; a zero read mid-service is fatal to the firmware (§4.5) [1][5].
- **Terminal Count clears only on counter reload** — a DMA NOP clears it, a plain NOP or an
  interrupt read does not (§2.3) [1][2].
- **Config registers must read back as written**; the HAL read-modify-writes Config 1/3
  (clearing the fast bit with an `and` at each disconnect, flipping $04 ↔ $07 around each
  DMA phase) and reads its own ID back out of Config 1 during reselection processing — on
  AMD silicon the own-ID field survives all resets (§2.3, §4.9) [1][2][5].
- **Phase bits are live** in normal operation (Features Enable/phase-latch off), and the
  firmware re-reads the phase from Status between every byte of its PIO loops (§4.7) [5].
- **A lone trailing byte of a 16-bit DMA read stays in the FIFO** (Save Residual Byte): DREQ
  is not asserted for it, the completion interrupt arrives with FIFO flags = 1, and the driver
  reads it out through the FIFO register. More than one leftover byte on a read is a fatal
  error to the driver (§4.7) [1][2][5].
- **The pseudo-DMA aperture bus-errors when unsatisfiable, and the firmware depends on it** —
  the SCSI Manager installs a bus-error handler before any blind transfer and resumes its
  loop from the fault (§3.8) [5][6].
- **The interrupt lines are levels, not latches, on PDM**: the AMIC IFR bits mirror the
  chip's INT and DREQ pins live; the VIA-style "clear" writes the HAL issues are required to
  be harmless for these bits (§3.3) [4][5].
- **Threshold-8 caps the sync offset at 7** on this design; Apple offers offset 7 even
  though its Threshold-8+Alternate-DMA combination would legally allow 15 (§4.10) [1][2][5].
- **The selection timeout is deliberately short**: Apple programs ≈ 203–216 ms instead of
  ANSI's 250 ms, with its own 7682 constant alongside the manuals' 8192 (§3.5) [1][2][5].
- **Enable Selection/Reselection must be re-issued after every disconnect** — the chip
  disables its selection/reselection circuit on disconnecting (§4.9) [2][5].
- **The Curio select-wedge erratum.** Apple's old-API select path contains a watchdog
  recovery for a hung Curio select: if the wait expires with Status reading $0A, an empty
  FIFO, sequence step $C1 *and* the Command register still reading back $C1 (the still-open
  DMA select), the firmware declares the chip wedged ("AMD shines again!") and fully
  reinitializes it [5]. The published AMD data sheet has no errata section and is silent;
  what it does establish is that the sequence-step register's upper bits are undefined on
  reads, that sequence step 1 is *not* a defined terminal state for Select-without-ATN, and
  that the FIFO is auto-cleared at the start of every selection — together making the
  observed signature a chip stalled mid-sequence in a state the data sheet says cannot
  persist (§2.3) [2] (*the wedge mechanism itself is observed, not explained by any
  datasheet*).
- **Command-register readback is top-of-queue**: the register returns the currently executing
  command, not the last written — indistinguishable in every path the Mac exercises, but the
  property the wedge check above probes (§2.3) [2][5].
- **Apple's equate collision**: the ROM's own header defines Receive Command Sequence as $29,
  the same opcode as Receive Command; the manual's value is $2B. Harmless — the Mac firmware
  never issues target receive sequences (§3.1) [1][5].
- **Curio silicon revisions are invisible through the SCSI cell** — the HAL's code knows two
  Curio revisions by the MACE Ethernet cell's chip-ID values ($0940 = rev B0, $0941 = rev A2)
  [5].
- **The 20-second cold-boot wait** on single-controller machines with default PRAM is
  authentic ROM behavior (a HAL feature-flag bug plus the default-PRAM fallback), not a
  hardware fault (§4.12) [5][7].
- **PDM prototypes differed**: behind a build flag, EVT1 boards report pseudo-DMA *without*
  the handshaked variant — a reminder that the aperture's handshake behavior is a property of
  the production AMIC, not of the technique (§3.8) [5].

## 6. Open questions

1. **DAFB Turbo SCSI register semantics** on the Quadra generation: the driver writes
   $1EC/$041 to $F9800024 and reads DREQ from a DAFB register at +$2, but the field layout of
   that register, and of the DAFB-side timing/control bits generally, is not established
   from the evidence here.
2. **Quadra-generation base addresses and board wiring**: Apple's driver takes them from
   low-memory globals seeded by platform decoder tables; the per-Quadra values are not in
   this page's evidence set, nor are Quadra 700/900/950 schematics.
3. **The Curio select-wedge erratum's mechanism**: observed only through Apple's workaround
   and signature check; no published erratum document describes it.
4. **AMIC SCSI-channel internals**: the internal buffering depth behind the 8-byte alignment
   rule and FLUSH semantics, the address register's readback behavior (never read by
   shipped code), and the exact scope of the RST bit are unobserved by software.
5. **Sync negotiation in practice on the 8100 fast bus**: the HAL fully supports it and
   offers 100 ns periods, but whether any shipped System 7 driver actually negotiates
   synchronous transfer there needs a trace on real hardware.
6. **Target role**: the firmware fields selection interrupts and reports
   selected-as-target, but the responding behavior of the SIM beyond polite acknowledgment
   is untraced here.
7. **Machine identities of some platforms in Apple's clock table**: "Zydeco" (SCSI clock
   24.28416 MHz, box flag "Eclipse") and "Wombat" (the BIOS-style SCSI Manager 96 platform)
   are named only in the driver sources; their product mapping is outside this evidence.
8. **6100/7100 SCSI clock wiring**: the Developer Note credits a 20 MHz oscillator shared
   with Ethernet; only the 8100's divided-40 MHz arrangement is observed in schematics. The
   smaller boards are *inferred* to be wired per the Developer Note.
9. **Undefined-space read values** at the Curio's $D0/$E0 slots: known only to be "not $A2";
   whether the value is stable is unestablished.
10. **The synchronous-period lookup table** in the HAL (factor → register value, with
    entries below 25 rejected) is observed as data and code behavior; its full contents and
    the meaning of the rejection tagging are not restated from a primary source.
11. **The exact byte-lane mechanics of 16-bit pseudo-DMA aperture accesses** inside AMIC
    (which internal path assembles the FIFO pair, and what a mixed-size access sequence
    does) — the driver's observed behavior fixes the order (first FIFO byte in the high
    half), not the implementation.
12. **The 20-second boot-wait chain** (§4.12): each link is observed in ROM code and
    behavior, but the feature-flag overwrite bug's history — whether it was ever noticed or
    fixed in a later ROM revision — is not established from this evidence.
