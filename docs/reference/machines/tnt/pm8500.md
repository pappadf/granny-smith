# Power Macintosh 8500/120

The Power Macintosh 8500/120 is the tower build of the TNT platform: the family's full audio-video configuration — video input *and* a second, television-bound video output stream — around a 120 MHz PowerPC 604 on the removable processor card, introduced in August 1995. Everything board-level is shared with the [Power Macintosh 7500](pm7500.md) and [Power Macintosh 9500](pm9500.md) and documented once in the family doc; this page carries only what is unique to the 8500.

## 1. Identity

### 1.1 What the machine is

The Power Macintosh 8500 "has a tower case design with room for expansion cards and an additional internal storage device" [1] §"Features of the Power Macintosh 8500" p. 9, and it is the machine on which the developer note concentrates the generation's audio-video story: built-in video input, and — unique in the family — a built-in second video output stream "for a television monitor or a VCR", able to "duplicate the main display (mirror mode) or display an independent image" [1] §"Second Stream Video Output" p. 22.

The subject of this page is the machine as shipped in August 1995: the **8500/120**, whose processor card carries a PowerPC 604 at 120 MHz. Apple's developer note documents the machine one speed grade lower — "a PowerPC 604 microprocessor running at a clock frequency of 100 MHz" [1] p. 9, the figure that recurs in its comparison tables [1] Table 1-3 p. 5 — which is the pre-announcement specification; no Apple document in the evidence corpus states the shipped 120 MHz rating or its bus ratio (§2.2, §6.1). The machine's later speed grades and the 1997 follow-ons are outside the corpus (§6.2).

The board is a TNT board in full: Hammerhead, two Bandit bridges, Grand Central and its Curio/MESH/AWACS/SWIM III cells, Chaos/Control/RaDACal video, the Cuda, and the AV parts (Plan B, the 8758/7196 input pair, Sixty6 and the 7187 DENC). The shared platform — buses, memory map, interrupt and clock architecture, the one 4 MB ROM — is the family doc's ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2–§5), and this page cites it rather than restating it.

### 1.4 Factory configurations

The documented configurations of the machine [1] Table 1-4 p. 11: 16 MB of DRAM in every one, an internal hard disk of 1 GB or 2 GB, and the internal CD-ROM drive present in both — the feature list calls the drive "optional" [1] p. 10 while every configuration row ships it. The 16 MB minimum is "installed in the form of two DIMMs" [1] §"Random-Access Memory" p. 16 — two 8 MB modules, the smallest same-size pair that both fills the minimum and enables the 128-bit interleave (§2.3) (*inferred — unverified* that the factory pair matches in size and type; §6.8).

### 2.2 Clocks on this machine

The clock architecture is the family's ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §5.4): the card supplies the system bus clock, PCI runs at 33 MHz asynchronously, the video bus is synchronous with the main system bus, and DRAM refresh is a CAS-before-RAS cycle every 15.6 µs. This machine's column of it:

| Clock | Value | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Processor core | 120 MHz (§1.1) | the shipping configuration; the note's 100 MHz is its pre-announcement figure [1] pp. 5, 9 |
| Processor (AR) bus | 40 MHz | *inferred — unverified*: the 9500 note's ratio law — the processor bus runs at "one-third the processor speed" on the 120 and 132 MHz parts [2] §"Bus Clock Rates" p. 11 — applied to 120 MHz; the 7500/8500 note's 50 MHz [1] §"Bus Bridge" p. 17 matches its 100 MHz specification, not the shipped card (§6.1) |
| Time base and decrementer | one quarter of the bus clock — 10 MHz | [3] §1.3.2.7.2: "In the 604, the time base frequency is 1/4th of the bus clock frequency (as is the decrementer frequency)" |
| PCI bus | 33 MHz, published by both bridge nodes as `clock-frequency` | [tnt.md](tnt.md) §5.4; [bandit.md](bandit.md) §1.5 |
| Video bus | synchronous with the main system bus | [1] §"Video Bus" p. 21; [tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.1 |
| SCSI cells | 53C94 at 25 MHz, MESH at 50 MHz | [tnt.md](tnt.md) §5.4 |
| AWACS sample clocks | the 44.1 kHz family by rate code | [tnt.md](tnt.md) §5.4; [grand-central.md](grand-central.md) §3.7 |

Because the bus clock is a property of the installed card ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.2), every rate downstream of the card — the AR bus, the time base and decrementer, the synchronous video bus — moves with a processor upgrade; the 33 MHz PCI clock and the two SCSI cell clocks do not.

### 2.3 Memory population and sizing

The Hammerhead law is the family's: DRAM on 8-byte (64-bit) DIMMs, a 128-bit interleave formed when the startup software finds two same-size DIMMs, physical memory made contiguous from `$00000000` by the bank base registers ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.3; [hammerhead.md](hammerhead.md) §2.10, §3.2). This machine's sockets and the rules that govern them:

- **Eight slots, labelled A1–A4 and B1–B4**; "slots with the same number constitute a slot pair" — A1/B1, A2/B2, A3/B3, A4/B4 [1] §"Installing RAM DIMMs" p. 36. DIMMs "can be installed one or more at a time", and the interleave forms when the startup software "detects two DIMMs that are the same size and type and installed in corresponding slots" [1] p. 36.
- **No soldered capacity**: "The Power Macintosh 7500 and 8500 computers do not have any main memory soldered to the logic board. At least one RAM DIMM must be present for the computer to operate" [1] p. 36.
- **Bank and module limits**: "The minimum bank size supported by the Hammerhead memory controller IC is 4 MB and the largest is 64 MB; the largest DIMM supported is a two-bank DIMM holding 128 MB" [1] §"RAM DIMM Configurations" p. 41. The 1 GB ceiling is reached with eight 128 MB DIMMs built from 64-megabit devices [1] pp. 16, 41 (Table 4-3 p. 41).
- **The DIMM itself**: an industry-standard 8-byte DIMM — mechanicals per JEDEC's MO-161 (of which this machine accepts only the shorter two of three heights, maximum 1.255 inches), electricals per JEDEC Standard 21-C, on a Burndy `ELF168E5GC-3Z50` connector [1] pp. 36, 43–44. The connector carries 64 data lines, a 14-bit address bus (`A(13:0)` with `B(0)` an alternate `A(0)` — tied to `A(0)` on this board, so the 64-bit bus is never split into 32-bit halves), four `/RAS` and eight `/CAS` strobes, and no +3.3 V power [1] Table 4-2 p. 38. The presence-detect (`PD(8:1)`, `/PDE`) and identification (`ID(1:0)`) pins defined by the JEDEC standard are **not used** [1] p. 38.
- **Device rules**: 1 MB, 4 MB, 16 MB and 64 MB devices supported, access time (T_RAS) 70 ns or less, at most two devices per data line and eight per `/RAS` line per DIMM [1] §"DRAM Devices" p. 43.
- **Address multiplexing**: Hammerhead supports two row/column multiplexing modes, selected per bank by control bits in the bank base registers as part of sizing; the mode table runs from the 4-megabit 256K×16 device (mode 0) to the 64-megabit 16M×4 device (mode 0), and "the memory controller does not support devices that require more than 12 row address bits" [1] §"RAM Address Multiplexing" pp. 41–42 (Tables 4-4, 4-5).
- **Sizing** is startup software's: it writes the mode bits while "determining the amount of RAM installed" [1] p. 41, over the register file of [hammerhead.md](hammerhead.md) §2.10. Mac OS, like every operating system on the platform, takes the memory size from the Open Firmware `/memory` node rather than by probing the controller.

### 2.4 The second-level cache as shipped

The family's L2 architecture — write-back, direct-mapped, allocate on read or write, synchronous-burst-SRAM data store, size sensed electrically rather than configured ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.3) — is fitted here with the family's only factory-installed removable cache: "The Power Macintosh 8500 computer comes with a 256 KB L2 cache SIMM installed" [1] §"Second-Level Cache" p. 17, expandable to 512 KB, 1 MB or 4 MB [1] §"Second-Level Cache SIMM" p. 45. The data-store SRAMs must be synchronous burst parts of 11 ns or less; the tag store is standard static RAM [1] p. 45.

The sensing path is the SIMM connector's: `CachePrsnt`, "active (low) if an L2 cache card is installed", and `CacheSize(1:0)` — "b00 for 512 KB, b01 for 256 KB, b10 for 1 MB, b11 for 4 MB" [1] Table 4-7 p. 49 — read by Hammerhead at startup through the two pins the note describes [1] p. 17 and surfaced in the register file as the presence bit and size code of [hammerhead.md](hammerhead.md) §2.8; the shipped 256 KB SIMM therefore presents code `b01` (§5). The connector also carries the processor data bus `ArDat(63:0)`, the buffered address `BufAdr(27:10)`, the tag store's value/valid/dirty bits and its enables, `CacheSysClk`, and both +5 V and +3.3 V power [1] Table 4-6 pp. 46–48, Table 4-7 p. 49.

The cache's timing behaviour is one of the places this machine differs from the 601 machines by silicon, and the developer note states the 604 side of it exactly [1] §"L2 Cache Operation" p. 45:

- "With only one processor, there is only one snooping mode so the ARTRY (address retry) signal is never asserted for a microprocessor access that hits in the L2 cache."
- "By using the DBDIS (data bus disable) signal defined for the PowerPC 604, the cache controller is able to assert DBG in the same cycle that TS asserts, so the processor can accept data (DBB) in the very next cycle."
- "The DRTRY (data retry) signal is never asserted, so the controller can use non-aging mode with the PowerPC 604."
- The consequence: "With the PowerPC 604, data is made available, that is, the TA (transfer acknowledge) signal is asserted, in the next clock cycle after the assertion of TS (transfer start). With the PowerPC 601, data is made available as early as the second clock cycle after the assertion of TS."

The L2 also allocates on reads from ROM addresses as well as main RAM [1] p. 45 — the whole Hammerhead-responded space — and the same SIMM design serves the 7500, "but with some differences in operation" [1] p. 45, of which the 604 signalling above is the documented difference.

## 3. Per-subsystem wiring

### 3.1 The processor card and the PowerPC 604

The card is the family's: "The main processor and its associated clock circuits are on a plug-in card. The processor card provides an upgrade path to a faster or more powerful microprocessor" [1] §"Main Processor" p. 14; the board acquires its bus clock from it ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.2). This machine's card carries the 604 — "the main processor in the Power Macintosh 8500 computer is a PowerPC 604 microprocessor" [1] p. 14 — with the feature set of §1.3: six parallel execution units, the on-chip MMU, and the split 16 KB instruction and data caches, four-way set associative [1] §"PowerPC 604 Microprocessor" p. 16.

The 604 makes the 8500 the machine the developer note writes compatibility copy for [1] §"PowerPC 604 Compatibility Issues" p. 12:

- **No POWER-only instructions.** The 601's instruction set included POWER instructions; "the PowerPC 604 microprocessor used in the Power Macintosh 8500 computer does not support the POWER-only instructions", so compilers must not emit them.
- **601 compatibility in software.** The machine "includes software emulation of some of the POWER features of the PowerPC 601 microprocessor" — user-mode 601 compatibility, not the POWER architecture — at a performance cost.
- **Split caches and code coherency.** "Whereas the PowerPC 601 microprocessor has a single cache for both instructions and data, the PowerPC 604 has separate instruction and data caches." Applications that generate code in memory and bypass the Code Fragment Manager without the proper cache-synchronization instructions "are likely to encounter problems when running on the PowerPC 604."

Firmware side, the CPU identity flows two ways: Open Firmware publishes what it found as the `AAPL,cpu-id` construction — which on this machine always carries the not-a-601 flag ([hammerhead.md](hammerhead.md) §4.3) — and the 68k half runs its CPU-identification dispatch at every boot ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.2). The 604's time base and decrementer tick at one quarter of the bus clock [3] §1.3.2.7.2 — 10 MHz at this machine's bus rate (§2.2).

### 3.2 The two Bandits and the PCI slot map

Bandit is the family's AR-to-PCI host bridge — buffering, address translation and the big/little-endian swap, 32-byte burst transfers ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §4.2; [bandit.md](bandit.md) §1.1). The 8500 carries **two** of them, and it is the only machine in the 1995 line for which both of these are true at once: two bridges *and* built-in video. The ROM's Open Firmware builds the tree from the identity registers, and for the `"AAPL,8500"` identity it emits both bridge nodes — `bandit@F2000000` (alias `pci1`) and `bandit@F4000000` (alias `pci2`) — each a `AAPL,343S1126` at its own 32 MB control slice, Bandit 2's the exact mirror of Bandit 1's with its PCI memory space forwarded from `$90000000` ([bandit.md](bandit.md) §1.4; [tnt.md](tnt.md) §3.2).

Grand Central sits behind Bandit 1, decoded at `$F3000000` inside that bridge's PCI memory pass-through window ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §3.1; [grand-central.md](grand-central.md) §1.3) — so the whole I/O stack, the NVRAM and the Sixty6 all live on Bandit 1's bus. The three PCI sockets are physical PCI slots, "connected directly to the PCI bus" [1] §"Bandit PCI Bridge IC" p. 18, at the industry-standard 33 MHz with 32-bit multiplexed address and data [1] §"PCI Expansion Slots" p. 49. **Which of the two buses carries them is not established by any source in evidence**: the developer note describes a single Bandit and one PCI bus for this machine [1] pp. 18, 49; the family index assigns the three sockets to Bandit 2 ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §6.2); and the only in-family published slot map — the 9500's — puts its A1/B1/C1 sockets at IDSEL 13, 14 and 15 on Bandit 1 ([bandit.md](bandit.md) §3.2.1). The 8500's own slot-to-bridge wiring, and whether Bandit 2's bus hosts anything at all beyond its bridge node, remain open (§6.3; [bandit.md](bandit.md) §6, item 5).

The bridge nodes carry Grand Central interrupt numbers 22 and 26 in the device tree ([bandit.md](bandit.md) §3.9) — the second of which is the same Grand Central line the built-in video's vertical blanking occupies on the TNT machines ([grand-central.md](grand-central.md) §3.2), an overlap whose resolution on this machine is undocumented (§6.4). PCI arbitration is external to the bridges — Grand Central highest, then the slots and Bandit master round-robin ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §5.3) — and the same arbitration serves both buses.

### 3.3 The display path: Control, Chaos, RaDACal, VRAM

The built-in display subsystem is the family's video architecture made concrete on this machine ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.4): Chaos buffers the video subsystem to the processor bus, Control provides addressing and control, RaDACal converts to analog and generates the video timing and the hardware cursor, and the framebuffer is VRAM on four SIMM slots — "each of which accepts a 1 MB VRAM SIMM" [1] §"Video Frame Buffer" p. 20. The data path through the video bus into the VRAM is 64 bits wide; the VRAM-to-RaDACal output path is 128 bits wide [1] p. 20. Chaos is a peer host bridge on its own `$F0000000` slice with the Control controller as its device-11 child; Open Firmware probes the display bus, sizes and assigns Control's two BARs — the 64 MB VRAM aperture at `$90000000` and the 4 KB register block at `$94000000` — and publishes them in the `control` node's `assigned-addresses` ([bandit.md](bandit.md) §3.10; *observed* in the ROM's own execution).

This machine's framebuffer budget, from the note's capability table [1] p. 20 and the features list [1] pp. 9–10:

| VRAM | Monitor reach | Second video stream |
|---|---|---|
| 2 MB (as shipped) | 24 bpp on monitors to 17 inch; 16 bpp on a 21-inch monitor | one display at a time — "when the second video stream is active, the main display shuts off" [1] p. 21 |
| 4 MB (all four SIMMs) | 24 bpp on all monitors to 21 inch | "the graphics monitor remains active at all times" [1] p. 21 |

The monitor connector is a DB-15 carrying R/G/B, sync and the three sense lines [1] §"Video Monitor Connector" pp. 29–30, identified through the Apple sense-code system — the standard codes for 12- and 14-inch RGB and the extended codes for the multiple-scan, VGA/SVGA and 16-inch families [1] Table 3-6 p. 30. VGA and SVGA share a sense code: the first start-up treats an SVGA monitor as VGA at 640×480, and the 800×600 selection sticks once made from the Monitors control panel [1] p. 30 note. Mode-setting the built-in display also runs through the Cuda: the pixel-clock synthesizer is programmed over the microcontroller's I²C passthrough (device address `$50`, registers 1–3), and a Cuda that does not answer that command blocks video mode changes — the mode-set path the ROM's own video driver depends on (*observed*; [4], [5]; §5). The ROM's native video driver mode-sets the built-in display through the Control registers early in the Mac OS start-up ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.7).

### 3.4 The AV input path: 8758, 7196, Plan B

Video input is implemented by the 8758 analog-to-digital converter and the 7196 digital video decoder and scaler (DESC) [1] §"Video Input" p. 21 — the composite and S-video input streams of the A/V panel (§4.2), NTSC, PAL and SECAM, with color-space conversion to 8 bpp grayscale, 16 bpp RGB, 16 bpp YUV or 32 bpp RGB and window resizing by decimation [1] Table 1-2 p. 4. "Video data can be stored either in the video display buffer (VRAM) or in an offscreen pixel map in main memory. Video data transfers are DMA transfers and are controlled by a DBDMA engine in the Plan B IC" [1] p. 21 — an engine separate from Grand Central's eleven channels, with its own two channels for the 7196 [1] p. 21 and the DBDMA command conventions of the family's engines ([dbdma.md](dbdma.md) §1.3):

- the **write channel** "takes data from the pixel FIFO buffer in the 7196 IC, attaches an appropriate DMA address, and performs a PCI write operation" — pixels into VRAM or main memory;
- the **read channel** "reads the 1-bit-per-pixel clip mask from main memory" [1] p. 21.

The read channel is what gives the machine its play-through mode: video written into the display buffer is clipped against the in-memory mask, providing "a video play-through mode with clipping of obscured regions and menus as well as simple titles" [1] p. 21. When the pixels land in an offscreen pixel map instead, "software can perform any required clipping and blending by using the CopyBits routine", and "the video input channel preserves the alpha channel so that software can perform alpha blending" [1] p. 21. The capture ceiling is the features list's: up to 24 bpp and up to 640×480 at 30 frames per second, for display, frame capture or QuickTime movie capture [1] p. 10. The 7196 can also take its input from the DAV connector [1] p. 21 (§4.2).

The input connector itself reaches outside the box: the S-video input socket is a 7-pin part whose pins 5 and 7 carry the I²C clock and data lines ("the Philips serial bus") and whose pin 6 supplies +12 V at up to 250 mA [1] Table 3-9 p. 34 — a bus and a supply that let an input-side device sit on the decoder's control path. Who masters that I²C bus, and how the 8758/7196 pair is programmed, is not documented in the note (§6.7).

In the firmware's tree the input engine appears as the `planb` node — `AAPL,343S1138`, `device_type "video-in"`, a child of Chaos alongside Control ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §4; [bandit.md](bandit.md) §3.10) — present on the AV machines only. Plan B's register geometry and its two channels' addresses are the family's least-documented ground ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §7, item 9; §6.6).

### 3.5 The second video output stream: Sixty6 and the 7187 DENC

The 8500's signature subsystem, present on no other machine in the family: "In addition to the main video display, the Power Macintosh 8500 computer provides a second video stream for a television monitor or a VCR" [1] §"Second Stream Video Output" p. 22. The stream is "a high-quality interlaced video signal with convolution to reduce interlace flicker", supporting NTSC or PAL television monitors, and it can mirror the main display or display an independent image [1] p. 22. The parts are the family roster's 8500-only pair ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.4, §4.5):

- **Sixty6** (343S1168) — "an RGB-to-YUV converter and convolver for the second video output stream" [1] §"Video Subsystem ICs" p. 20: "converts the video data from RGB color space to YUV color space and then performs the convolution on the data in YUV color space" [1] p. 22. It lives behind Grand Central at `+$1C000`, on the controller's 16-bit side bus, and interrupts on Grand Central source 27 ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §4; [grand-central.md](grand-central.md) §2.5, §3.2) — classified by the kernel into emulated 68k level 2 ([grand-central.md](grand-central.md) §3.4). The ROM carries a native driver for it (`.Display_Video_Apple_Sixty6`) plus an NTSC/PAL gamma table [4].
- **the 7187 DENC** — "a digital video encoder for the second video output stream" [1] p. 20, which "takes square pixels in YUV format from the Sixty6 IC and encodes them into composite video in either NTSC or PAL format" [1] p. 22.

The VRAM budget for dual-stream operation is §3.3's table: with the full 4 MB, monitor and television run simultaneously — a 24 bpp second stream leaves up to 16 bpp on a 21-inch monitor, a 16 bpp second stream leaves up to 24 bpp [1] p. 21. With 2 MB the second stream blanks the main display. The generation's video feature table also credits this machine (and the 7500) with genlock support and dual-stream output "with all pixel depths" — where the first-generation AV machines convolved only up to 8 bpp [1] Table 1-2 p. 4.

The output reaches the outside world on the A/V panel: a standard 4-pin S-video output socket and an RCA composite output, whose signal "is the same as that on pin 5 of the S-video output connector" [1] §"Composite Video Connectors" p. 34, alongside the stereo RCA audio pair [1] §"Audio and Video Ports" p. 33.

### 3.6 Storage: MESH, the 53C94, and the drives

The two-bus SCSI architecture is the family's ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §4.3): MESH, the internal controller, runs the internal bus at up to 10 MB/s "because this bus does not have to drive a long external bus", while the external bus through Curio's 53C94 runs at up to 5 MB/s [1] §"MESH High-Speed SCSI Interface" p. 19. Both cells are decoded through Grand Central (MESH at `+$18000` on DBDMA channel 10, the 53C94 at `+$10000` on channel 0) and both appear in this machine's TNT decoder record ([grand-central.md](grand-central.md) §2.5; [dbdma.md](dbdma.md) §1.4; [tnt.md](tnt.md) §3.6).

The drive allocation, per the note:

- **The internal hard disk is on the internal (MESH) bus**: "The hard disk drive is connected to the internal SCSI bus" [1] §"Internal Hard Disk Drive" p. 31, the 10 MB/s bus of the features list [1] p. 10. Capacity is 1 or 2 GB depending on configuration [1] p. 31.
- **The internal CD-ROM drive sits on the external bus's internal end**: the machine has "a SCSI bus for external SCSI devices and for the internal CD-ROM drive", with the external connector a 25-pin D-type and "the internal CD-ROM drive uses a 50-pin connector" [1] §"SCSI Port" p. 27. "The internal end of the SCSI bus is terminated by a 110 Ω passive terminator... located on the main logic board near the portion of the internal chassis connector that contains the signals for the internal CD-ROM drive. The internal CD-ROM drive does not include a terminator" [1] pp. 27, 31. The external connector has automatic termination — active with no external device connected, removed when one is [1] p. 27.
- **The CD-ROM itself** is an AppleCD 600i: quadruple-speed, 600 KB/s sustained, under 200 ms average access, over 3 MB/s SCSI burst, reading CD-ROM, CD-ROM XA, CD-I and PhotoCD plus audio [1] §"CD-ROM Drive" p. 31.
- **The floppy** is the internal 1.4 MB Apple SuperDrive on a 20-pin connector, driven by SWIM III on DBDMA channel 1 [1] §"Floppy Disk Drive" p. 32; [dbdma.md](dbdma.md) §1.4.

### 3.7 Communications and sound

- **Serial.** Two ESCC channels (Curio's SCC section, with 8-byte FIFOs on both transmit and receive [1] §"Curio I/O Controller IC" p. 19) on 9-pin circular mini-DIN sockets that accept 8- or 9-pin plugs [1] §"Serial Ports" p. 25. Either port handles asynchronous or synchronous traffic including AppleTalk and the GeoPort protocols; the handshake input takes an external clock to 920 Kbit/s, and each port's GPi pin feeds the corresponding SCC data-carrier-detect input [1] Table 3-1 p. 25, p. 25. Pin 9 supplies +5 V, within a 500 mA budget shared with the ADB port's supply; "excessive current drain causes a circuit breaker to interrupt the +5 V supply; the breaker automatically resets when the load returns to normal" [1] pp. 25–26.
- **Ethernet.** The MACE core in Curio, brought to "a built-in Ethernet port [that] accepts either a 10baseT cable or the Apple Ethernet adapter for thicknet or thinnet cables" — two connectors, "operates only one of them at a time", defaulting to 10baseT if both are occupied [1] §"Ethernet Port" p. 26. The station address lives in the ID PROM behind Grand Central ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §4; [grand-central.md](grand-central.md) §2.5).
- **Sound.** AWACS — the waveform amplifier plus 16-bit codec conforming to the IT&T ASCO 2300 specification [1] §"AWAC Sound IC" p. 19 — on DBDMA channels 8 (output) and 9 (input) ([dbdma.md](dbdma.md) §1.4). Digitization and recording at 16 bits and up to 44.1 kHz [1] §"Sound Input Jack" p. 28. The sound input jack is a 1/8-inch stereo phone jack with a fourth contact supplying power to an Apple PlainTalk microphone (input impedance 8 kΩ, maximum level 2 V rms, maximum gain 22.5 dB, signal-to-noise 82 dB) [1] pp. 28–29; the output jack mutes the internal speaker when plugged (output impedance 37 Ω, 0.9 V rms maximum, 20 Hz–20 kHz ±2 dB, less than 0.05 percent harmonic distortion plus noise, 85 dB signal-to-noise, 80 dB channel separation) [1] §"Sound Output Jack" p. 29. The A/V panel repeats the audio pair on RCA connectors [1] p. 34. A PCI card can inject digital audio into AWACS through the DAV connector [1] pp. 19, 51 (§4.2).

### 3.8 Cuda, ADB, and the nonvolatile store

The system-management microcontroller, its VIA1 transport, the nonvolatile store and the real-time clock are the family's ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §4.3, §5.5; [grand-central.md](grand-central.md) §2.5–§2.6): the Cuda holds soft power, reset management, parameter RAM, ADB and the clock, and this machine adds to its duties the video pixel-clock I²C programming of §3.3. The ADB reaches the outside on a single 4-pin mini-DIN port [1] §"Apple Desktop Bus (ADB) Port" p. 26 — a single-master, multiple-slave bus whose pin 2, PSW, is the "power-on signal (generates reset and interrupt key combinations)" [1] Table 3-2 p. 26. The port's +5 V pin shares the 500 mA budget with the serial ports' pin 9 [1] p. 26.

## 4. Expansion

### 4.1 The three PCI slots

- **The bus.** "The Power Macintosh 7500 and 8500 computers have three expansion slots using the industry standard PCI bus. The PCI bus is a nonsplit bus with 32-bit multiplexed address and data. The PCI expansion slots in these computers use a 33 MHz system clock" [1] §"PCI Expansion Slots" p. 49. The cards are "standard PCI cards as defined by the *PCI Local Bus Specification*, Revision 2.0", required "to use the 5 V signaling standard and to use the standard ISA fence" [1] p. 49.
- **Power.** "Each computer provides a total of 50 W of power for its three expansion slots. Both 5 V and 3.3 V are supplied; the total power at both voltages must not exceed the 50 W maximum" [1] p. 49.
- **Signals.** The slots support the required PCI signal set — `AD(31:0)`, `C/BE(3:0)`, `PAR`, `/FRAME`, `/TRDY`, `/IRDY`, `/STOP`, `/DEVSEL`, `/IDSEL`, `/REQ`, `/GNT`, `CLK`, `/RST` and the four interrupt pins "wired together on each slot" [1] Table 4-8 p. 50. They do **not** support the 64-bit bus extension, the cache-support signals, JTAG boundary scan, `/LOCK`, or the error-reporting pair `/PERR` and `/SERR` [1] p. 50.
- **Configuration and BARs.** Cards are identified and assigned by Open Firmware through the bridges' configuration ports ([bandit.md](bandit.md) §2, §4.2); the bridge whose bus carries a socket walks IDSEL 13/14/15 for three devices, the pattern the 9500's published `slot-names` establishes for its A1/B1/C1 triple ([bandit.md](bandit.md) §3.2.1) — the 8500's own socket numbering is not published by any source in evidence (§6.3).
- **Interrupts.** Each socket's INTA–INTD lines are OR-combined and collected by Grand Central on its external lines ([bandit.md](bandit.md) §1.3, §3.9); the 9500's published external table is the family's precedent for which lines slots take ([grand-central.md](grand-central.md) §3.2), and this machine's own per-slot assignment is unpublished (§6.5).

### 4.2 The DAV connector

- **What it is.** "Audio and video signals on the main circuit board are accessible through the DAV connector, which is located near one of the PCI expansion card connectors. A PCI expansion card can pick up the A/V signals by means of a cable and plug for the DAV connector. The user can install one such card in the computer" [1] §"DAV Connector" p. 51. Unlike the first generation, "the new Power Macintosh computers do not have an AV card; instead, all the A/V circuitry is located on the main logic board" [1] p. 51 note.
- **What it carries.** The connector "taps into the computer's video and sound data streams, providing access to the 4:2:2 unscaled digital video input signal and the digital audio input signal for the system's sound encoder/decoder (codec)" — so a card can capture or generate these streams "without passing them through the PCI bus", the architecture of a hardware compression card: raw data through the DAV interface, compressed data over PCI [1] §"The DAV Interface" p. 51. The 7196 DESC can accept its video input from the connector [1] §"Video Input" p. 21, and a card can transmit digital audio to the AWACS IC through it [1] pp. 19, 51. The interface differs from the first generation's NuBus-era DAV [1] p. 51 note; Apple's *Power Macintosh DAV Interface for PCI Expansion Cards* — named by the note as the specification, in preparation at the time — is the document a card designer needs [1] §"Supplemental Reference Documents" p. x.
- **The connector.** A 60-pin dual-row part with 0.100-inch pin spacing, pin 1 across from pin 31 [1] §"Signals on the DAV Connector" p. 52. Its signals are the Y and UV nibbles of the 4:2:2 stream, the line-locked clock and its qualifier, vertical sync and HRef, a direction pin, the I²C clock and data pair, the analog and digital audio input pairs, the digital audio output, sync and clock, and the S-video input Y and C components [1] Table 4-9 pp. 52–53.

### 4.3 Internal storage bays

"The computer has space for three additional internal drives: one 5.25-inch drive (optional CD-ROM occupies this space) and two 3.5-inch drives" [1] §"Features of the Power Macintosh 8500" p. 10 — one 3.5-inch bay beyond the 7500's [1] Table 1-3 p. 5. The hard disk is 1 or 2 GB [1] p. 10; the CD-ROM option is the AppleCD 600i (§3.6); the floppy occupies its own bay [1] p. 10.

### 4.4 What this machine does not expand by

- **No NuBus.** "The Power Macintosh 7500 and 8500 computers use PCI cards for expansion and do not have any NuBus expansion slots" — the escape hatch is "an external NuBus expansion chassis with two or three NuBus slots... connected by a cable to one of the computer's PCI slots", within which Slot Manager calls function normally [1] §"NuBus Expansion Cards" p. 11.
- **No Slot Manager.** Without the chassis, "calls to the Slot Manager in the Power Macintosh 7500 and 8500 computers return an error result"; display-card software is directed to the Display Manager instead [1] §"Slot Manager Compatibility" p. 11.
- **Ceilings.** RAM tops out at 1 GB (§2.3), VRAM at 4 MB (§3.3), the L2 at 4 MB (§2.4), and the DAV path serves one card (§4.2). The processor upgrade path is the card itself ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.2).

## 5. Boot sequence summary

The boot contract is the family's — Open Firmware performs the hardware bring-up, `HWInit` is only the POST diagnostics object, and the default `boot-command` loads the Mac OS boot path out of the ROM itself ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.7; [hammerhead.md](hammerhead.md) §4.2). The sequence below walks that contract naming this machine's branch points; everything unmarked is the family doc's:

1. **Reset.** The 604 fetches the hardware-reset vector at `$FFF00100`, inside Hammerhead's ROM decode, and enters the NanoKernel's reset entry ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.7 step 1). The kernel builds the BAT and page-table mappings from the ROM's configuration record, including the 604-relevant I/O attribute handling ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §3.5).
2. **Open Firmware.** The identity decode reads the Hammerhead identifier (`$39`), the machine-class flag (bit 31 set) and BoxID (bit 13 clear) and selects `"AAPL,8500"` ([hammerhead.md](hammerhead.md) §4.3). Because this machine's CPU is a 604, the `AAPL,cpu-id` construction carries the not-a-601 flag ([hammerhead.md](hammerhead.md) §4.3) — the value operating systems use to tell an 8500 from a 7500 beyond the `compatible` string. The firmware builds the device tree with both Bandit nodes, the Chaos bus, and the display nodes — it probes the display bus, sizes and assigns Control's BARs, and publishes the `assigned-addresses` of §3.3 (*observed*; [bandit.md](bandit.md) §3.10) — plus the `planb` and `sixty6` AV nodes, sizes memory through Hammerhead, formats NVRAM if the store is blank, and sets up `aliases`, `options` and `chosen` ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.7 step 3).
3. **POST.** The diagnostics object runs; its L2 test reads the presence bit — set on this machine, whose cache SIMM is factory-installed (§2.4) — decodes size code 1 (256 KB), walks the cache-sized memory range under the flush/fill strobe, and writes the enable word ([hammerhead.md](hammerhead.md) §4.4; *observed* on this machine's personality). Results are logged into NVRAM through Grand Central's bank-select and data apertures ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.7 step 4).
4. **`boot /AAPL,ROM`.** The 68k emulator starts the Toolbox half of the image. Its machine identification reads the same registers the firmware read, synthesizes the 8500's software CPU ID from Hammerhead bit-state and BoxID bit 11, and selects this machine's product record — product kind `$3F`, gestalt 69 (*observed*; [hammerhead.md](hammerhead.md) §4.3) — installing the TNT decoder record and the 8500's RAM-info and vector tables. The 68k half also runs its CPU-identification dispatch for the 604 ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.2).
5. **Mac OS start-up.** Low-memory setup, driver loading, then the device-tree import into the Name Registry — a conversation of roughly eight hundred kernel-service rounds between the NanoKernel and the 68k emulator's reflector (*observed* in the ROM's execution on this platform; [4]). The boot path waits on the boot chime's DMA transfer: the kernel spins on a byte-reversed read of the audio-output DBDMA channel's status register (`$F3008804`), testing the ACTIVE bit, and does not proceed until the transfer drains (*observed* in the kernel disassembly; [4]; the same wait runs on every machine in the family). The ROM's native video driver mode-sets the built-in display through the Control registers, programming the pixel clock through the Cuda's I²C path (§3.3) ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.7).
6. **Boot-device search.** The Start Manager honours the PRAM default boot device first, falling back to a full drive-queue walk only after a spin-up timeout of roughly thirty guest-seconds — a machine with no disk at the recorded ID looks stalled for that long before it proceeds (*observed* on cold boots from zeroed PRAM; [tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.7). The internal hard disk answers on the MESH bus through DBDMA channel 10 (§3.6).

## 6. Open questions

1. **The shipping speed grade's documentation.** No Apple document in the evidence corpus states the 8500/120's 120 MHz core rate or its bus ratio; the developer note's 100 MHz and 50 MHz figures [1] pp. 5, 9, 17 are its pre-announcement specification. The 40 MHz bus of §2.2 is *inferred — unverified* from the 9500 note's one-third ratio law [2] p. 11 and the 604's quarter-bus timebase [3] §1.3.2.7.2; a ROM or Apple document naming the shipped card's clocks would settle it.
2. **The later speed grades.** The 8500 line was sold at higher core rates after 1995; none of those configurations, their cards, or any ROM they shipped is in the evidence corpus, and whether they changed `bus-frequency` or only the core multiplier is unknown.
3. **The PCI sockets' bridge.** Three sockets, two bridges, no assignment: the note describes one Bandit and one PCI bus [1] pp. 18, 49; the family index places the sockets on Bandit 2 ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §6.2); the 9500's published `slot-names` put its A1/B1/C1 triple on Bandit 1 ([bandit.md](bandit.md) §3.2.1). Whether the 8500's sockets sit on Bandit 1 or Bandit 2, and what else (if anything) Bandit 2's bus hosts, is unresolved (bandit.md §6, item 5).
4. **Bandit 2's interrupt line.** The bridge nodes declare Grand Central numbers 22 and 26 ([bandit.md](bandit.md) §3.9), but 26 is also the built-in video's vertical-blanking line on the TNT machines ([grand-central.md](grand-central.md) §3.2). How a two-bridge machine with built-in video reconciles the two consumers of the same line is not described by any source.
5. **The per-slot interrupt assignment.** The 9500's published external table is the only in-family slot-to-line map ([grand-central.md](grand-central.md) §3.2); the 8500's three sockets' lines are unpublished, and the 23–25 triple is a precedent, not an attestation.
6. **Sixty6 and Plan B register surfaces.** The two AV parts' part numbers, tree nodes, interrupt and driver are ROM-attested ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §4; §3.4, §3.5), but neither register file — nor the addresses of Plan B's two DBDMA channels — is established from primary material (tnt.md §7, item 9).
7. **The video-input control bus.** The S-video input connector carries I²C clock and data [1] Table 3-9 p. 34, and the 8758/7196 pair needs a control path; whether that bus is the Cuda's I²C passthrough (as the pixel clock is, §3.3), mastered by Plan B, or driven by the 7196 itself is unattested.
8. **The factory memory load-out.** The 16 MB minimum is "two DIMMs" [1] p. 16; that they are a same-size, same-type pair in corresponding slots (so the machine leaves the factory interleaved) is *inferred — unverified* — the note documents the rule, not the shipped configuration.
9. **The capture budget.** The note gives no data-rate or bandwidth figures for the video-input path on this machine — no analogue of the earlier AV machines' megabytes-per-second figures — so the real-time capture ceiling beyond the 640×480-at-30-frames headline [1] p. 10 is not established.
10. **The machine's codename.** The TNT platform name is ROM-attested ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §1.1); the 8500's own project codename appears in no Apple document in the corpus, and community attributions are not citable here.
11. **BoxID beyond bits 11 and 13.** The two decodes consume only BoxID bits 11 and 13 ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.6); this machine's remaining BoxID bits — the factory-test strap included, whose set state detours the boot into the ROM's serial test monitor ([grand-central.md](grand-central.md) §2.5) — are unattested, so the register's full power-on value on a real 8500 is unknown.
12. **The processor card interface.** The card supplies the bus clock and carries the CPU ([tnt.md](tnt.md) §2.2), but no document in evidence gives its connector, its signals, or how firmware learns the installed card's speed (tnt.md §7, item 12) — the upgrade mechanism is documented as a capability, not as an interface.
